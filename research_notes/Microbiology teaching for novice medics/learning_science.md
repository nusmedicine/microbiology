## 1. Cognitive load theory (CLT): element interactivity, expertise reversal, segmenting, pre-training

### Takeaway
Novices have limited working memory. Microbiology is high in element interactivity: many organisms × many attributes (cell wall, oxygen use, genome type, envelope, replication site, transmission, virulence factors), and the attributes interact (for example, a Gram-negative outer membrane → LPS → endotoxin → the host response that general pathology covers). CLT therefore argues for (a) pre-training the "attribute vocabulary" before showing organisms, (b) segmenting and sequencing from low to high interactivity, and (c) fading support as expertise grows (expertise reversal). Segmenting and pre-training have median effect sizes of about d ≈ 0.75–0.8 in Mayer's lab-based multimedia studies.

### Cited Findings
- van Merriënboer & Sweller (2010) set out CLT design guidelines for health-professions education. CLT assumes a limited working memory and an effectively unlimited long-term memory that holds schemas. It distinguishes intrinsic, extraneous and germane load, and aims to cut extraneous load while managing intrinsic load. *Medical Education* 44(1):85–93. [V] — [van Merriënboer & Sweller 2010, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03498.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03498.x); summary record at [Maastricht CRIS](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/6fb15293-27ff-4d0a-abff-301f3794a8b9)
- AMEE Guide No. 86 (Young, van Merriënboer, Durning & ten Cate 2014) applies CLT to medical education. *Med Teach* 36(5):371–384. [V: citation] — [Young et al. 2014, doi:10.3109/0142159X.2014.889290](https://doi.org/10.3109/0142159X.2014.889290)
- Leppink & van den Heuvel (2015) review how CLT has developed in medical education. They define intrinsic load through element interactivity, the number of elements that must be processed together in working memory. [B] — [Leppink & van den Heuvel 2015, Perspect Med Educ, doi:10.1007/s40037-015-0192-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40037-015-0192-x)
- Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017) argue that the expertise reversal effect is a variant of a more general element-interactivity effect. What counts as "high interactivity" depends on the learner's existing schemas, because schemas chunk elements together. [B] — [Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2017, Educ Psychol Rev 29:393–405, doi:10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1)
- Expertise reversal effect: guidance that helps novices (worked examples, integrated explanations) loses its advantage, or becomes harmful, as learners gain knowledge. In trade apprentices, worked examples beat problem solving early, and the advantage reversed with expertise. [V: secondary] — [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, Educ Psychol 38(1):23–31, doi:10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4); summary in [Wikipedia: Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)
- Segmenting principle: supported in 10/10 experimental tests, median d = 0.79. Pre-training principle: supported in 13/16 tests, median d = 0.75. Lab studies, mostly short causal explanations such as how lightning forms or how brakes work. [V: secondary, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning] — [Cambridge Core, Mayer chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258). An informal University of Michigan deck reports different medians (segmenting 0.98 from 3 studies; pre-training 0.85 from 5 studies), which reflects different study samples — [UM slide deck](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/um-multimedialearning-final.pdf)
- Mayer (2010) translated the science of learning for medical educators, including CLT-consistent multimedia principles. [B] — [Mayer 2010, Med Educ 44(6):543–549, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2010.03624.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2010.03624.x). Mayer (2020) adds 10 evidence-based multimedia principles for anatomy instruction — [Mayer 2020, Clinical Anatomy, via VUMC PDF](https://www.vumc.org/faculty/sites/default/files/Medical%20Education/Multimedia%20approach%20to%20medical%20edu.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Pre-training applied to microbiology.** Before any organism appears, give a short, self-contained "attribute toolkit" chapter. Teach each attribute dimension once, with its mechanism and a "so what": Gram-positive vs Gram-negative envelope; obligate aerobe, facultative anaerobe, obligate anaerobe (linked to oxidative stress, catalase and superoxide dismutase); spore formation; capsule; DNA vs RNA virus, enveloped vs naked; yeast vs mould; protozoa vs helminths. Each dimension becomes one chunk. Organisms can then be learned as combinations of known chunks rather than as new isolated facts. This is pre-training in Mayer's sense and sets up schema-based learning (Section 2).
- **Managing element interactivity.** Do not present an organism with all its attributes at once. Sequence the content as (1) single attribute dimensions in isolation (isolated-elements approach), (2) two-dimension combinations, for example a 2×2 of Gram stain × oxygen requirement, (3) full profiles. In the Svelte UI, use progressive disclosure: collapsed attribute rows that learners expand, and toggles that reveal one dimension at a time on a comparison grid.
- **Segmenting.** Split mechanism animations into learner-paced steps with "Next" buttons, for example a viral replication cycle: attachment → entry → uncoating → genome replication → assembly → release. Each segment should carry one idea.
- **Fading for expertise reversal.** Year-1 students start as true novices, but by the end of the block they are not. Fade scaffolds within the book. Early chapters give fully worked "organism profile" walkthroughs. Later chapters give partially completed profiles. Final review gives blank profiles or "which organism fits?" problems. Include an optional "skip worked example" path for students who pass a short pre-test, an adaptive response to expertise reversal.
- **Extraneous load.** Avoid long scrolling catalogue tables with 20+ columns. Keep labels on diagrams rather than in separate legends (spatial contiguity), and avoid decorative images (coherence).

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly measures cognitive load or element interactivity for microbiology content.
- The segmenting and pre-training effect sizes come from short, lab-based multimedia lessons. I found no trial of pre-training in a medical microbiology course.
- I did not obtain the AMEE Guide 86 full text to list its specific recommendations.

## 2. Schema building and expert knowledge organisation: illness scripts, encapsulation, "microbe profiles" for pre-clinical novices

### Takeaway
Expert clinicians store disease knowledge as illness scripts (enabling conditions → fault → consequences). Biomedical knowledge such as microbiology becomes "encapsulated" under higher-level concepts through repeated application to cases. Pre-clinical novices cannot yet build full illness scripts. They can build the causal, biomedical substrate that later encapsulates. Experimental med-ed evidence (Woods, Norman and colleagues) shows that teaching causal mechanisms improves *delayed* retention of diagnostic categories compared with teaching feature lists. That supports a mechanism-first "microbe profile" structure over a fact catalogue.

### Cited Findings
- Knowledge encapsulation (Schmidt & Boshuizen): biomedical knowledge becomes "encapsulated" into higher-level clinical concepts that act as simplified causal models of signs and symptoms. This develops mainly through repeated application of biomedical knowledge to patient problems. [V: secondary] — [Rikers, Schmidt & Boshuizen 2000 record (Utrecht)](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/knowledge-encapsulation-and-the-intermediate-effect/); [Schmidt & Boshuizen 1993, Educ Psychol Rev 5:205–221, doi:10.1007/BF01323044](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01323044) [B]
- Rikers, Schmidt & Boshuizen (2000): the proportion of encapsulating concepts in recall and pathophysiological explanations rose with expertise level. [V: abstract] — [Rikers et al. 2000, Contemp Educ Psychol](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/knowledge-encapsulation-and-the-intermediate-effect/)
- Schmidt & Rikers (2007) describe the developmental sequence: (1) elaborated causal networks of biomedical knowledge, then (2) encapsulation through clinical experience, then (3) illness scripts, then (4) instance scripts. [B] — [Schmidt & Rikers 2007, Med Educ 41(12):1133–1139, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02915.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02915.x)
- Custers' 30-year review of illness scripts: the concept came from cognitive psychology, entered medicine through AI diagnostic systems, and was refined by medical educators. It is well grounded and underpins tools such as the Script Concordance Test. The standard script components are enabling conditions, fault (pathophysiology) and consequences (signs and symptoms). [V] — [Custers 2015, Med Teach 37(5):457–462, doi:10.3109/0142159X.2014.956052](https://doi.org/10.3109/0142159X.2014.956052); [PDF](https://wikiMSK.org/w/img_auth.php/a/a9/Custers2014_Thirty_years_of_illness_scripts.pdf)
- **Causal basic science improves delayed retention (key med-ed evidence).** Woods, Brooks & Norman (2007), Exp 1: students who learned a causal model kept diagnostic performance better after a delay (89% vs 78%). Exp 2: after a delay, students relied more on causal information (57% vs 43%). Conclusion: knowing the causal mechanism helps memory for diagnostic categories. [V: abstract] — [Woods, Brooks & Norman 2007, Adv Health Sci Educ 12(4), doi:10.1007/s10459-006-9055-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10459-006-9055-x)
- Woods et al. (2006, Academic Medicine): accuracy was the same immediately after learning (70% in both groups). One week later, the group taught epidemiological information dropped to 51%, while the group taught basic-science mechanisms dropped only to 62%. [V: secondary summary] — [McMaster Experts record / search summary](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1608618)
- Lisk, Agur & Woods (2016): novices who learned integrated causal mechanisms showed better diagnostic accuracy and a better grasp of the relative importance of key clinical features. [V: abstract] — [Perspect Med Educ, doi:10.1007/s40037-016-0268-2](https://reference-global.com/article/10.1007/S40037-016-0268-2)
- "Cognitive integration": basic science helps most when it is taught as a causal explanation of clinical features, not as a parallel fact set. [B] — [Kulasegaram, Martimianakis, Mylopoulos, Whitehead & Woods 2013, Acad Med 88(10):1578–1585, doi:10.1097/ACM.0b013e3182a45def](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0b013e3182a45def)
- Microbiology-specific (non-peer-reviewed): a Harvard essay describing a pathogen database built for first-year medical students argues that medical microbiology feels like "an endless list" unless students see an organising logic. It organises pathogens by phylogeny, morphology, ecology and pathology. This is an undated essay, not a peer-reviewed study. — [Harvard IMID database essay](https://imid.med.harvard.edu/database_essay.txt)
- The ASM curriculum guidelines for introductory microbiology organise content around a small set of concepts and "fundamental statements" (evolution, cell structure and function, metabolic pathways, information flow, microbial systems, impact of microorganisms) to promote understanding over memorisation. The Microbiology Concept Inventory was later validated against these. [B] — [Merkel 2012, JMBE 13(1):32–38, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v13i1.363](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v13i1.363); [Paustian et al. 2017, JMBE 18(3), doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1320](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1320)

### Inferences
- **"Microbe profile" as a pre-clinical proto-script.** Give every organism the same fixed template so students build one reusable schema rather than many idiosyncratic ones. The template should map onto the illness-script slots students will use later, while staying within the brief (no sites, diagnosis or treatment):
  - *Identity / structure* (what it is): domain or group, envelope or cell wall, genome type, morphology. This maps to the later "fault" (agent).
  - *Lifestyle / niche* (enabling conditions, at a general level): oxygen relationship, reservoir (human, animal, environment), how it is transmitted in general terms, intracellular vs extracellular.
  - *Mechanism of harm* (fault): the one or two signature virulence strategies, such as toxin, capsule or intracellular survival, linked to the general-pathology concepts taught next (necrosis, inflammation).
  - *Signature consequence* (consequence, kept general): for example "toxin-mediated disease without invasion" vs "pyogenic, invasive", without systems-level detail.
  - *Why it matters / contrast partner*: which organism it is most easily confused with, and the attribute that separates them (links to Section 3).
- **Mechanism first, list second.** The Woods et al. findings suggest that each attribute should be paired with a "because". Examples: "Clostridium is an obligate anaerobe *because* it lacks enzymes to detoxify reactive oxygen species, *so* it thrives in devitalised, poorly perfused tissue." "Enveloped viruses are *more* fragile in the environment *because* the lipid envelope is disrupted by drying and detergents, *so* transmission tends to need close contact or fluids." Causal links like these are what later become encapsulated knowledge.
- **Fit with the curriculum sequence.** The book precedes immunology, microbiology proper and pharmacology, and follows general pathology. Each profile's "mechanism of harm" can link explicitly back to general pathology (inflammation types, granuloma, necrosis). That deliberately builds causal integration, the substrate that encapsulates later.
- **Build profiles in the UI.** Students fill in profile slots themselves (generation, Section 9) and receive feedback. A "profile builder" Svelte component (drag attributes into slots, then compare with the expert profile) is an interactive form of schema construction.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study of "pathogen scripts" or "microbe profiles" as an instructional intervention for pre-clinical students. This is an inference from illness-script and cognitive-integration theory.
- The Woods et al. studies used clinical disorders (neurological, rheumatological), not infectious diseases. Transfer of the effect to microbiology is assumed.
- I did not retrieve the Schmidt & Rikers 2007 full text this session (citation from known literature).

## 3. Comparison and contrast: analogical encoding, contrasting cases, side-by-side organism comparison

### Takeaway
Actively comparing two or more cases yields better learning and transfer than studying the same cases one at a time: about d ≈ 0.50 across 57 experiments. The benefit is largest for perceptual content, and when the underlying principle is given *after* the comparison. Classic analogical-encoding studies show large transfer gains from comparison. Simultaneous side-by-side presentation produces discriminative contrast comparable to interleaving. For microbiology, this argues for structured comparison of "near-neighbour" organism pairs (for example *Staphylococcus* vs *Streptococcus*, enveloped vs naked viruses, yeast vs mould), not one-organism-per-page reading.

### Cited Findings
- Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013) meta-analysis: 57 experiments, 336 tests. Case comparison activities generally produced better learning than other forms of case study (sequential or single case), d = 0.50 (random effects). Moderators: comparison objectives, how the principle was presented, content type, and test timing. Giving the principle *after* the comparison helped more than giving it before or not at all. Perceptual content showed the largest benefits. Prompting for similarities alone may sometimes beat prompting for both similarities and differences. [V: abstract and summary] — [Alfieri et al. 2013, Educ Psychol 48(2):87–113, doi:10.1080/00461520.2013.775712; PDF](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/ContrastingCasesMeta-AlfieriEtAl2013.pdf)
- Analogical encoding: Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003) showed that comparing two cases yields better schema abstraction and transfer than studying the same cases separately. [V: abstract] — [Gentner et al. 2003, J Educ Psychol 95(2):393–408, doi:10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393](https://dedregentner.owlstown.net/publications/38525-learning-and-transfer-a-general-role-for-analogical-encoding)
- Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner (1999): students who compared two cases were nearly three times more likely to transfer the target strategy than students who read the same cases separately. Note: the "three times" figure belongs to this 1999 paper, not the 2003 one. [V] — [Loewenstein et al. 1999, Psychon Bull Rev](https://kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/1999/analogical-encoding-facilitates-knowledge-transfer-in-negotiation?p=1)
- In negotiation teams, separate analysis of cases was no better than no training. Comparison was required for transfer. [V] — [Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner 2003, AMLE](https://business.illinois.edu/loewenstein/papers/Loewensteinetal%20AMLE03.pdf)
- Contrasting cases and "A time for telling": analysing contrasting cases *before* a lecture prepared students to learn more from the lecture than reading or summarising a text did. The value of telling depends on prior differentiation of the problem space. [B] — [Schwartz & Bransford 1998, Cognition and Instruction 16(4):475–522, doi:10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4)
- Kang & Pashler (2012): when exemplars from different categories (painting styles) were shown *simultaneously*, learning matched interleaved presentation and beat blocked presentation. This supports the idea that interleaving works through discriminative contrast, which side-by-side display also provides. [B] — [Kang & Pashler 2012, Appl Cogn Psychol 26:97–103, doi:10.1002/acp.1801](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1801)
- Medical education: in ECG interpretation, students who practised with mixed categories and contrasted competing diagnoses outperformed students who practised by blocked category (Hatala, Brooks & Norman 2003). [B] — [Hatala et al. 2003, Adv Health Sci Educ 8:17–26, doi:10.1023/A:1022687404380](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022687404380)

### Inferences
- **Comparison tables need active processing.** A static comparison table on its own is "studying" rather than "comparing". The meta-analytic benefit comes from comparison *activities*. Build interactive comparison widgets in which students must (a) predict which attribute differs, (b) fill in empty cells, or (c) state the one attribute that discriminates the pair. Then reveal the principle (principle-after-comparison).
- **Pick near-neighbour pairs** that share most attributes and differ on one or two diagnostic, mechanistically meaningful features:
  - *Staphylococcus* vs *Streptococcus*: both Gram-positive cocci. They differ in arrangement (clusters vs chains) and catalase. The catalase difference links back to the oxygen-tolerance concept.
  - Obligate aerobe vs facultative anaerobe vs obligate anaerobe: three organisms side by side, with the same thioglycolate-tube image, where students predict where growth appears.
  - Enveloped vs non-enveloped virus: an influenza-like model vs a norovirus-like model. Students predict environmental stability and disinfectant susceptibility.
  - Yeast vs mould (*Candida* vs *Aspergillus*) and protozoan vs helminth: unicellular vs multicellular eukaryotes, with implications for size, life cycle and how they are seen.
  - Gram-positive vs Gram-negative envelope, overlaid in a single diagram (outer membrane, LPS, peptidoglycan thickness).
- **Diversity without a catalogue.** Instead of 50 organism pages, teach "contrast sets" (around 6–10 well-chosen pairs or triads). Each illustrates a dimension of diversity. Students learn the *dimensions* through comparison, and individual organisms serve as exemplars.
- **Contrasting cases as a preparation for "telling"** (Schwartz & Bransford): open a chapter with two growth curves, two Gram-stain images or two outbreak vignettes. Ask "what is different and why might that be?" before the explanatory text.

### Gaps
- I found no study directly comparing side-by-side vs one-at-a-time study of *microorganisms* (organism profiles) in medical or microbiology education.
- The Alfieri meta-analysis spans many domains (mostly not medicine). The d = 0.50 figure is an overall estimate. A secondary summary on the same page reported 0.60, so the exact figure should be checked in the full text.

## 4. Interleaving vs blocking for category learning, applied to discriminating organism categories

### Takeaway
Interleaving exemplars of different categories gives a moderate benefit for inductive category learning (g = 0.42 overall; g = 0.67 for paintings and other visual material). The benefit is larger when categories are *similar to each other* and variable internally. Blocking is better for word lists (g = −0.39), and effects for expository text are ambiguous. Learners consistently misjudge blocking as more effective. Medical-image studies (ECG, chest radiography) support interleaving for novices, with a caveat that some initial mastery may be needed first. For microbiology, interleave *practice* on discriminating visually or conceptually similar categories (Gram stain images, morphology, viral structures, growth patterns), not the initial expository explanations.

### Cited Findings
- Brunmair & Richter (2019) meta-analysis: 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples. Overall interleaving effect Hedges' g = 0.42. Paintings g = 0.67, along with other visual materials. Mathematical tasks g = 0.34. Expository texts and tastes: ambiguous, nonsignificant. Words: blocking advantage g = −0.39. Meta-regression: interleaving effects are stronger when material is *more similar between* categories, *less similar within* categories, and more complex. The authors warn that interleaving "should be used with caution" for expository texts and words. [V: full-text preprint] — [Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psychol Bull 145(11), doi:10.1037/bul0000209; preprint PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Kornell & Bjork (2008): interleaving paintings by different artists improved the classification of new paintings compared with blocking. Yet most participants believed blocking had helped them more (a metacognitive illusion). [B] — [Kornell & Bjork 2008, Psychol Sci 19(6):585–592, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x). Since then, dozens of studies have replicated the interleaved-category-learning advantage, including with bird species. [V: secondary review chapter] — [Kang, interleaved training and category learning (UNH)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-interleaved-training-and-category-learning-kang.pdf)
- Mechanism: interleaving helps mainly by highlighting *differences between* categories (discriminative contrast), with some role for retrieval and spacing. [B] — [Birnbaum, Kornell, Bjork & Bjork 2013, Mem Cognit 41:392–402, doi:10.3758/s13421-012-0272-7](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0272-7); category structure matters: interleaving benefits low-discriminability (similar) categories, while blocking can help when within-category similarity is low and categories are easy to tell apart — [Carvalho & Goldstone 2014, Mem Cognit 42:481–495, doi:10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0) [B]
- Classroom evidence (mathematics): a randomised controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice in US 7th grade found a large benefit on a delayed test (d ≈ 0.83). [B; exact figure to verify] — [Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020, J Educ Psychol 112(1):40–52, doi:10.1037/edu0000367](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367)
- Medical education, radiology: 40 medical students without radiology training were randomised to massed (11 single-disease blocks) or interleaved (3 blocks of randomised images) chest radiograph patterns. Interleaved scored higher, about 57% vs 43% overall in one report. Another report gives about 61% vs 46% for recall and 53% vs 40% for induction (published in J Am Coll Radiol, lead author Sherry X. Yan). [V: abstract and news reports; author listing differs between sources] — [RSNA 2014 abstract](https://archive.rsna.org/2014/14002540.html); [Radiology Business](https://radiologybusiness.com/topics/healthcare-management/leadership/med-students-respond-better-spaced-out-education-strategy)
- Medical education, ECG: a 2×2 study with 80 first-year medical students crossed instruction schedule (massed or distributed) with practice schedule (interleaved or blocked). The authors suggest interleaved practice may only become effective after enough initial mastery. [V: snippet] — [Perspect Med Educ, doi:10.1007/s40037-017-0365-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40037-017-0365-x); see also [Hatala, Brooks & Norman 2003, doi:10.1023/A:1022687404380](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022687404380) [B]
- Histopathology: an adaptive perceptual-learning module (Kellman's PALM) with 261 skin histopathology images, which spaced and sequenced categories by each learner's accuracy and response time, improved accuracy and fluency in medical students. This tests adaptive interleaved sequencing, not a clean interleaved-vs-blocked comparison. [V: abstract] — [Krasne, Hillman, Kellman & Drake 2013, J Pathol Inform 4:34, PMC3908489](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3908489)
- Microbiology course (undergraduate): the "U-Behavior" intervention (LMS-integrated coaching with visualisations of quiz-attempt patterns) substantially increased students' spacing and interleaving of retrieval practice (ds > 1.75) without raising total quiz attempts. Only about half of students reached optimal practice levels. The excerpt did not report a direct retention outcome. [V: abstract] — [Access Microbiology, doi:10.1099/acmi.0.001226](https://www.microbiologyresearch.org/content/journal/acmi/10.1099/acmi.0.001226.v2)

### Inferences
- **Where to interleave in microbiology.** The categories most similar to one another, and so with the highest interleaving payoff, are perceptual or feature-based discriminations:
  - Gram-stain and morphology images (Gram-positive cocci in clusters vs chains; Gram-negative rods vs coccobacilli; yeast vs pseudohyphae vs hyphae).
  - Growth patterns (thioglycolate tube position; haemolysis types on blood agar, as a basic-science illustration of toxins rather than as diagnostics).
  - Viral architecture (enveloped or naked × DNA or RNA × capsid symmetry).
  - Parasite forms (protozoan trophozoite vs cyst; helminth classes).
  Build an "Image sorter" Svelte component that draws randomly across categories. Give feedback that names the discriminating feature, not just right or wrong.
- **Where *not* to interleave.** Initial expository explanations (for example, the chapter explaining the Gram-negative envelope) should stay blocked and coherent. Brunmair & Richter found no reliable interleaving benefit for expository text, and the ECG study suggests some mastery first. Pattern: *block to learn, interleave to practise and discriminate.*
- **End-of-chapter and cumulative quizzes** should mix earlier categories. A virology quiz can include bacteria-vs-virus discrimination items, so students must choose the right category, not only recall within a known category.
- **Warn students about the metacognitive illusion.** Kornell & Bjork's participants preferred blocking even though it worked worse. A short "why this quiz feels harder" note can reduce frustration and opt-outs.

### Gaps
- I found no interleaving study using microbiology content (for example Gram-stain images or organism classification).
- I found no clean interleaved-vs-blocked RCT in dermatology or histology images. The available evidence is radiology, ECG, and adaptive perceptual-learning studies.
- The Rohrer 2020 d value is from memory of the paper and should be verified.

## 5. Retrieval practice and spacing in medical education; spaced-repetition flashcards; writing conceptual retrieval questions

### Takeaway
Practice testing and distributed practice are the two techniques rated "high utility" by Dunlosky et al. (2013). Meta-analyses give testing effects of g ≈ 0.5–0.6 overall and about 0.5 in real classrooms. RCTs in medical education show durable gains, for example at 6 months in residents, and Cohen's d ≈ 0.7–1.0 for spaced e-mailed cases at 6–11 months in students. Evidence that Anki use *causes* better outcomes is correlational and small: about 1 Step 1 point per ~1,700 unique cards. Rote fact cards are a real risk for conceptual material. Questions should target "why/how", application and discrimination, and should transfer to new examples.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) rated 10 techniques. **High utility:** practice testing and distributed practice. **Moderate:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. **Low:** summarisation, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. [B] — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychol Sci Public Interest 14(1):4–58, doi:10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)
- Testing-effect meta-analyses: Rowland (2014) g = 0.50 [B] — [Psychol Bull 140(6):1432–1463, doi:10.1037/a0037559](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559). Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017) g = 0.61 overall [B] — [Rev Educ Res 87(3):659–701, doi:10.3102/0034654316689306](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306). Yang et al. (2021), classroom quizzing, g ≈ 0.50 [B] — [Psychol Bull 147(4):399–435, doi:10.1037/bul0000309](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)
- Transfer of test-enhanced learning: retrieval practice transfers to new contexts and question formats, but less to different content. Transfer is stronger when retrieval questions are elaborate and include feedback. Overall d ≈ 0.40. [B] — [Pan & Rickard 2018, Psychol Bull 144(7):710–756, doi:10.1037/bul0000151](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)
- Spacing meta-analysis: 839 assessments across 317 experiments showed robust spaced-over-massed benefits. The optimal gap grows with the retention interval (roughly 10–20% of the interval). [B] — [Cepeda et al. 2006, Psychol Bull 132(3):354–380, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)
- Medical education: Larsen, Butler & Roediger (2008) reviewed test-enhanced learning in medical education [B] — [Med Educ 42(10):959–966, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03124.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03124.x). Their 2009 RCT in paediatric/emergency residents (within-subjects, counterbalanced) compared repeated testing with repeated study of a teaching-conference topic, with two more sessions at 2-week intervals. On a final test 6 months later, retrieval beat restudy. The authors note restudy was a stronger control than typical resident behaviour. [V: secondary] — [Larsen, Butler & Roediger 2009, Med Educ 43(12):1174–1181, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03518.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03518.x); [Learning Scientists summary](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2023/3/30)
- Review of test-enhanced learning in contemporary medical education. [V: title] — [PMC9005759](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9005759/)
- Spaced education RCT (Kerfoot et al. 2007): medical students received weekly e-mailed case scenarios with questions on 2 of 4 urology topics. 95 of 133 (71%) completed the end-of-year test. Spaced education significantly improved composite scores (p < 0.001), with Cohen's d = 1.01 and 0.73 for students whose urology teaching was 6–8 and 9–11 months earlier. [V: abstract] — [Kerfoot et al. 2007, Med Educ 41(1):23–31, PubMed 17209889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17209889/). A follow-up multi-institution RCT (n = 115) improved retention for one topic pair (PC/PSA, p = 0.04) but not another (BPH/ED, p = 0.60). [V] — [Kerfoot & Brotschi 2009, PubMed 18614145](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614145/)
- **Anki (correlational).** Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015), n = 72 at one school: the number of unique Anki cards seen independently predicted USMLE Step 1 score. Each additional ~1,700 unique Anki cards or ~445 board-style questions was associated with about 1 extra Step 1 point, controlling for other factors. Commercial Firecracker cards did not predict scores. A 2016 erratum corrected a coefficient. [V] — [Deng et al. 2015, Perspect Med Educ 4:308–313, PMC4673073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673073)
- Anki, retrospective cohort (osteopathic school, 2023): 35 Anki users vs 268 non-users. Step 1 means 223.71 vs 222.58. Failure rate 2.8% vs 10.94%, not statistically significant. No significant link between Anki use and scores. [V: abstract] — [IJMS 2023](https://ijms.pitt.edu/IJMS/article/view/1549). Wothe et al. (2023, J Med Educ Curric Dev) surveyed Anki use against academic and wellness outcomes. I did not retrieve its results. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/7aaf357db5d54ad39fea3f672ad5aae4)
- Unsuccessful retrieval attempts followed by feedback still enhance learning (the "errorful generation" benefit). [B] — [Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009, J Exp Psychol LMC 35(4):989–998, doi:10.1037/a0015729](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729)
- Flashcard spacing vs cramming: spacing a larger stack beat massed smaller stacks for 90% of participants, yet most believed massing was more effective. [B] — [Kornell 2009, Appl Cogn Psychol 23:1297–1317, doi:10.1002/acp.1537](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1537)
- Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation (both "moderate utility" in Dunlosky 2013). Self-explanation meta-analysis: g = 0.55. [B] — [Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne 2018, Educ Psychol Rev 30:703–725, doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x). Medical education: self-explanation while solving clinical cases improved medical students' diagnostic performance on new cases. [B] — [Chamberland et al. 2011, Med Educ 45(7):688–695, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2011.03933.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2011.03933.x)

### Inferences
- **Spaced-repetition (SRS) deck design for conceptual microbiology.** Avoid "Organism X → 8 facts" cards. Prefer:
  - *Why/how cards* (elaborative interrogation): "Why does a thick peptidoglycan layer retain crystal violet after alcohol decolourisation?" "Why are obligate anaerobes killed by oxygen?"
  - *Discrimination cards* (interleaving built in): show two organisms or images and ask "What single attribute separates them, and why does it matter?"
  - *Prediction / application cards* with novel exemplars: "A newly discovered virus is inactivated by ether. What does this tell you about its structure?" This tests transfer, which Pan & Rickard found depends on elaborate retrieval.
  - *Cloze cards limited to vocabulary* during pre-training only (for example "Facultative anaerobes can generate ATP by ___ in the absence of O₂").
  - One idea per card. Each card links back to the relevant e-book section for feedback ("see why").
- **Spacing within a 3-month block.** With roughly 12 weeks to the final assessment, Cepeda's ratio suggests review gaps of about 1–2 weeks for core concepts. An SRS algorithm handles this per card. The book should also build in *cumulative* quizzes at chapter ends that resurface earlier chapters.
- **Feedback with explanation.** Every quiz item should give elaborated feedback (the mechanism) after an attempt. Errors with feedback are beneficial (Kornell et al. 2009). This also makes "retrieval" double as a causal-explanation opportunity (Woods; Section 2).
- **Risks of rote flashcards.** The Deng data are correlational, and the effect is modest. Students who use pre-made, high-volume decks may optimise fact recall at the expense of schema building. Keep the book's deck small (high-yield concepts and discriminations) and make the conceptual cards prominent.
- **Exportable decks.** If students use Anki, offer an exportable deck that contains the conceptual card types, so their existing habits reinforce the book's schema rather than compete with it.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of spaced repetition or retrieval practice specifically for *microbiology* in medical students. The U-Behavior microbiology study changed *practice behaviours*, and its learning outcome was not retrieved.
- I found no experimental evidence comparing conceptual vs factual flashcards in medical students. The "risk of rote flashcards" point is an inference from transfer research (Pan & Rickard) and Dunlosky's ratings, not a direct finding.
- I did not retrieve the exact effect size of Larsen et al. 2009.

## 6. Worked examples vs productive failure; PBL vs direct instruction for novices; when to use cases

### Takeaway
For true novices learning new, high-interactivity material, fully guided instruction (worked examples, explicit explanation) is more efficient for acquiring knowledge (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006; expertise reversal). Productive failure (problem solving *before* instruction) has a moderate advantage for *conceptual understanding and transfer* (g = 0.36 overall; larger with high design fidelity). It works only when followed by explicit instruction that builds on students' attempts, and not for very young learners. A health-professions RCT found productive failure equal on knowledge and better on "preparation for future learning". Practical synthesis: short, low-stakes "try first" problems that activate and expose prior ideas, then explicit worked explanation, then guided cases with fading. No unguided discovery.

### Cited Findings
- Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006): minimally guided instruction (discovery, problem-based, inquiry) is less effective and less efficient than guided instruction for novices, because it ignores working-memory limits. The advantage of guidance shrinks when learners have enough prior knowledge. [B] — [Kirschner et al. 2006, Educ Psychol 41(2):75–86, doi:10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1)
- Sinha & Kapur (2021) meta-analysis: 53 studies, 166 comparisons of problem solving followed by instruction (PS-I) vs instruction followed by problem solving (I-PS). PS-I was favoured with a significant moderate effect, Hedges' g = 0.36 [95% CI 0.20, 0.51]. Effects were larger with high fidelity to productive-failure design principles (reported g up to about 0.58). A publication-bias-adjusted estimate reported in one version was higher (g ≈ 0.87, treat with caution). Effects were negative for grades 2–5, possibly because of low design fidelity in those studies. Benefits concentrate on conceptual knowledge and transfer, not procedural knowledge. [V: abstract via secondary sources and PDF mirror] — [Sinha & Kapur 2021, Rev Educ Res 91(5):761–798, doi:10.3102/00346543211019105](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/352523653_When_Problem_Solving_Followed_by_Instruction_Works_Evidence_for_Productive_Failure); [Frontiers review citing figures](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.956416/full)
- Comparing preparatory activities: scaffolding the initial problem solving did not differ significantly from unscaffolded PS-I (g = −0.08 [−0.20, 0.04]). Problem solving compared with an alternative sense-making activity was non-significant (g = 0.22 [−0.06, 0.51]). [V] — [Sinha & Kapur, "From problem-solving to sensemaking" (ETH)](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/492056)
- Health professions RCT: 40 Year-1 PharmD students (University of Toronto) were randomised to productive failure or direct instruction alone. Knowledge acquisition and application did not differ. Productive failure outperformed on a preparation-for-future-learning test. [V: abstract] — [Steenhof, Woods, Van Gerven & Mylopoulos 2019, Adv Health Sci Educ 24(4):739–749](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/productive-failure-as-an-instructional-approach-to-promote-future/); related mechanism paper — [UHN news](https://www.uhnresearch.ca/news/3-01-2021/rewards-that-come-with-failure)
- PBL meta-analysis (Dochy et al. 2003): PBL showed a small *negative* trend on knowledge and a positive effect on skills. Knowledge differences shrank or reversed over time (retention). [B] — [Dochy, Segers, Van den Bossche & Gijbels 2003, Learn Instr 13(5):533–568, doi:10.1016/S0959-4752(02)00025-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0959-4752(02)00025-7)
- Medical education worked examples: case-based worked examples in a computer-supported environment, including erroneous examples with feedback, improved medical students' diagnostic knowledge. [B] — [Kopp, Stark & Fischer 2008, Med Educ 42(8):823–829, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03122.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03122.x)
- Microbiology case-based teaching for Year-1 students: an interactive, case-based microbiology laboratory session for first-year medical students, where small groups use Gram stain and biochemical tests on short cases (conference abstract, 2025/26). [V: title and summary] — [PMC12792493](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12792493/)

### Inferences
- **"Try first" microbiology problems** (productive-failure lite, with low stakes and no grades):
  - "Here are growth results for three organisms in tubes with an oxygen gradient. Propose a rule that explains them." Then the explicit explanation of aerobic/anaerobic metabolism and ROS detoxification, with the expert answer contrasted against typical student answers.
  - "Two viruses: one survives on a doorknob for days, the other for minutes. Hypothesise a structural reason." Then the enveloped/naked explanation.
  - "Why might a bacterium that lives inside host cells be hard for antibodies to reach?" A preview that bridges to the immunology block.
  Productive-failure design fidelity matters. The problem must be accessible with prior knowledge (cell biology), allow several solution ideas, and be *followed* by instruction that explicitly compares student ideas with the canonical one.
- **Branching cases: use them for consolidation, not first contact.** Given Kirschner et al. and expertise reversal, branching cases come *after* the attribute toolkit and profile chapters, and should be scaffolded. Keep cases within scope: "an organism with these properties is found; reason about what it can do", focused on microbial properties and mechanisms rather than diagnosis or treatment. Start with worked-example cases (an expert's reasoning narrated step by step), then completion cases (some steps blank), then full branching cases.
- **Erroneous examples.** "Here is a student's organism profile with two errors. Find them." This is effective (Kopp et al.) and pairs naturally with comparison.

### Gaps
- I found no productive-failure study using microbiology content, and none with medical (as opposed to pharmacy) students.
- The Sinha & Kapur fidelity-adjusted and bias-adjusted figures came from secondary reproductions. Exact moderator values (conceptual vs procedural vs transfer) should be checked in the published paper.

## 7. Concept mapping in medical and microbiology education

### Takeaway
Concept maps have a moderate learning benefit (g = 0.58 across 142 effect sizes). *Constructing* maps (g = 0.72) is better than *studying* expert maps (g = 0.43). Med-ed reviews describe four uses: meaningful learning, an added resource, instructor feedback, and assessment. Microbiology-specific studies (bacteriology courses, TBL sessions) are small, quasi-experimental and mostly positive. Student satisfaction is mixed.

### Cited Findings
- Daley & Torre (2010) reviewed 35 studies of concept maps in medical education. They identified four functions: promoting meaningful learning, serving as an additional learning resource, enabling instructor feedback, and assessing learning and performance. [V: PubMed record] — [Daley & Torre 2010, Med Educ 44(5):440–448, PubMed 20374475](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20374475/); later 12 tips — [Twelve tips for teaching with concept maps in medical education](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/twelve-tips-for-teaching-with-concept-maps-in-medical-education/)
- Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018) meta-analysis: 142 effect sizes, n = 11,814. Overall g = 0.58 for learning with concept and knowledge maps. Constructing maps g = 0.72; studying maps g = 0.43. [V: secondary summaries] — [Schroeder et al. 2018, Educ Psychol Rev 30:431–455, doi:10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)
- Earlier meta-analysis: Nesbit & Adesope 2006. [B] — [Rev Educ Res 76(3):413–448, doi:10.3102/00346543076003413](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543076003413)
- Microbiology, Iran: a quasi-experimental study of 89 general medical students in a bacteriology course (34 concept-map, 55 control) tested whether concept maps improved learning. [V: abstract; result direction reported as positive in summary] — [Research in Medical Education (GUMS)](https://rme.gums.ac.ir/article-1-1433-en.pdf)
- Microbiology, China (2013): electronic concept maps in a microbiology course gave significantly higher total and term-explanation scores than controls. Most students said the maps helped understanding and memory. [V: abstract] — [Microbiology China](https://wsws.ijournals.cn/wswxtbcn/article/pdf/tb13112115)
- Medical school TBL (Oakland University William Beaumont, 2025): collaborative concept mapping in a TBL session on HIV and *Pneumocystis jirovecii* across three cohorts of first-year medical students (n = 375). The maps helped teams synthesise pre-class and in-class information. [V: abstract] — [Frontiers in Education 2025, doi:10.3389/feduc.2025.1604406](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1604406/full)
- Undergraduate lecture-laboratory microbiology: conceptual and procedural knowledge mapping. [V: title] — [ECU thesis](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/handle/10342/4112)

### Inferences
- **Partially completed maps.** Offer "skeleton" concept maps that students complete, for example a map linking *Gram-negative envelope → outer membrane → LPS → lipid A → endotoxin → host inflammation (general pathology)*. This fits the construct > study finding and keeps load manageable for novices (a scaffolded version of construction).
- **One map per "dimension of diversity"**, not per organism. Examples: an oxygen-relationship map, an envelope map, a viral-genome-to-replication-strategy map. This reinforces organising concepts over catalogue content.
- **Assessment and feedback.** A Svelte drag-to-link component can auto-check key propositions (node-link-node triples) and give feedback. This is the "instructor feedback" function Daley & Torre describe.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of concept mapping in medical microbiology. The available microbiology studies are quasi-experimental and single-site, and the Iranian study's effect size was not retrieved.
- I found no evidence on *digital* auto-graded concept maps vs paper maps in medical education.

## 8. Multimedia learning principles and interactive e-textbooks or simulations

### Takeaway
Mayer's principles (coherence, signalling, spatial and temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, personalisation) are well supported in lab studies and have been translated for medical educators. Evidence for interactive courseware ("doer effect") suggests that doing interactive activities is associated with much larger learning gains than reading or watching: about 6× reading and 3× video per unit of activity in CMU OLI courses. Causal estimates come from quasi-experimental analyses by the developers' group.

### Cited Findings
- Mayer's multimedia principles were translated for medical education [B] — [Mayer 2010, Med Educ, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2923.2010.03624.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2010.03624.x), and summarised as 10 research-based principles for anatomy instruction — [Mayer 2020 Clinical Anatomy, VUMC PDF](https://www.vumc.org/faculty/sites/default/files/Medical%20Education/Multimedia%20approach%20to%20medical%20edu.pdf)
- Segmenting median d = 0.79 (10/10 tests). Pre-training median d = 0.75 (13/16 tests). [V: secondary] — [Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258)
- A critical view argues that some of Mayer's evidence shows text-based explanation is often as good as or better than elaborate media (a blog critique, not peer reviewed). — [Sadki 2025 blog](https://redasadki.me/2025/09/13/richard-mayers-research-on-multimedia-for-learning-actually-proves-text-works-better/)
- Doer effect: in CMU Open Learning Initiative courseware, the number of interactive practice activities completed was associated with learning outcomes about 6× more strongly than pages read and about 3× more than videos watched. [V: secondary summaries] — [Koedinger, Kim, Jia, McLaughlin & Bier 2015, "Learning is not a spectator sport", L@S, doi:10.1145/2724660.2724681](https://cdl.ucf.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Doer-Effect.pdf)
- Koedinger et al. (2016, LAK) used analytics rather than randomisation to argue a causal link: doing within a unit predicts learning of that unit more than doing in other units. They acknowledge that RCTs would be more rigorous. [V] — [par.nsf.gov PDF](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10277323)
- Replication across seven courses (15.2 million events, 18,546 students) found doing was causally related to learning on unit tests and the final exam, using causal-inference methods. This is from the developers' commercial partner (VitalSource), and I found no independent replication. [V] — [VitalSource research](https://research.vitalsource.com/publications/the-doer-effect-at-scale-investigating-correlation-and-causation-across-seven-courses)
- ICAP framework: learning increases from Passive → Active → Constructive → Interactive engagement. [B] — [Chi & Wylie 2014, Educ Psychol 49(4):219–243, doi:10.1080/00461520.2014.965823](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823)
- Active learning in undergraduate STEM: exam scores improved by about 0.47 SD, and failure was 1.5× more likely under traditional lecturing. [B] — [Freeman et al. 2014, PNAS 111(23):8410–8415, doi:10.1073/pnas.1319030111](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111)

### Inferences
- **Embed an activity every 1–3 screens of text** (doer effect, ICAP "constructive"). Examples: a one-question predict-and-reveal, a drag-to-classify, or a "complete the profile" cell. Avoid long passive reading.
- **Concrete multimedia rules for the book:**
  - *Spatial contiguity:* label the Gram-positive/Gram-negative envelope diagram in place, and use hover tooltips rather than a separate key.
  - *Signalling:* colour-code attributes consistently across the whole book (for example, one colour always means "envelope", another "oxygen"), so the comparison grid, profiles and diagrams share a visual vocabulary.
  - *Coherence:* no decorative microbe clip-art. Use authentic micrographs or Gram-stain images only where discrimination is the goal.
  - *Segmenting:* step-through animations of replication cycles and toxin mechanisms.
  - *Pre-training:* a glossary or "toolkit" chapter first.
  - *Modality:* optional short narrated animations for complex dynamic processes; avoid on-screen text that duplicates narration (redundancy).
- **Simulations.** A "virtual culture" simulation (choose oxygen conditions, temperature or media, then observe growth) is a constructive activity that teaches mechanism without diagnostic detail.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed RCT of interactive e-textbooks in medical or biology education specifically. The doer-effect evidence is from general courseware, mostly by one research group and its commercial partner.
- I did not search for biology-simulation evidence (for example PhET or HHMI BioInteractive) in depth this session.

## 9. Desirable difficulties, generation effect, prediction prompts ("predict before reveal")

### Takeaway
Making learning effortful in the *right* ways (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, generation, prediction) improves long-term retention and transfer, even though it lowers performance during learning and learners misjudge it. Generating answers gives about d ≈ 0.40. Pretesting and prequestions improve later learning of the tested content. Prediction works best when followed by immediate feedback that produces a "surprise" (prediction error). For novices, difficulties are desirable only if learners can respond successfully. Otherwise they become undesirable load.

### Cited Findings
- Learning vs performance distinction: conditions that slow acquisition (spacing, interleaving, testing) often improve long-term retention and transfer, and learners routinely mistake fluency for learning. [B] — [Soderstrom & Bjork 2015, Perspect Psychol Sci 10(2):176–199, doi:10.1177/1745691615569000](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)
- Generation effect meta-analysis: 86 studies, mean d ≈ 0.40. Larger at longer delays. [B] — [Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel 2007, Mem Cognit 35(2):201–210, doi:10.3758/BF03193441](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441)
- Brod (2021) review, "Predicting as a learning strategy": generating a prediction before seeing the correct answer improves learning. The proposed mechanisms are curiosity and attention, and prediction-error ("surprise") driven updating when feedback follows. Prediction needs feedback, and the mechanisms are still being clarified. [V: title and abstract] — [Brod 2021, Psychon Bull Rev, doi:10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642250/)
- Breitwieser & Brod (2021): children (9–11 years) remembered more facts after generating predictions than examples. In adults (17–29 years, n = 25) both strategies were similarly effective. [V] — [Child Development 2021, pedocs](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2022/25234/)
- Pretesting: unsuccessful attempts to answer questions *before* studying improved later learning compared with extra study time. [B] — [Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009, J Exp Psychol Appl 15(3):243–257, doi:10.1037/a0016496](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496); errors with feedback still help — [Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009, doi:10.1037/a0015729](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729) [B]
- Interleaving and spacing as desirable difficulties: see Sections 4–5 ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [Cepeda et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)).
- Boundary condition for novices: high-interactivity material plus too much difficulty overloads working memory (CLT; expertise reversal). [B] — [Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4); [Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1)

### Inferences
- **"Predict before reveal" components (Svelte), within scope and answerable from cell-biology prior knowledge:**
  - *Gram stain:* "After the alcohol decolouriser, which cell keeps the purple dye: thick peptidoglycan or thin peptidoglycan plus an outer membrane?" Then reveal the animation.
  - *Oxygen:* "Where in a tube with an oxygen gradient will an organism lacking catalase and superoxide dismutase grow?" Then reveal the tube image.
  - *Enveloped virus:* "Will hand sanitiser (alcohol) inactivate this virus?" Then reveal and explain lipid disruption, contrasted with a naked virus.
  - *Spores:* "After boiling for 10 minutes, which culture regrows?"
  - *Viral genome:* "A (+)ssRNA virus enters a cell. Can its genome be translated immediately? What about a (−)ssRNA virus?" This sets up a mechanistic contrast (the need to package an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase).
  Log predictions, then show the answer with a short causal explanation. This captures prediction-error learning and also gives the instructor analytics on common misconceptions.
- **Pre-questions at chapter start**: 2–3 questions answered before reading. They are not graded, and students are told that wrong answers are expected and useful.
- **Calibrate difficulty.** Prediction prompts must be answerable by *reasoning* from existing knowledge, not guessing unknown facts. "Predict the name of the toxin" is undesirable. "Predict whether a toxin-secreting organism needs to invade tissue to cause harm" is desirable.
- **Metacognitive messaging.** Tell students explicitly that interleaved and spaced quizzes feel harder but work better (Soderstrom & Bjork; Kornell & Bjork). This reduces fluency-driven opt-outs.

### Gaps
- I found no prediction-prompt or generation-effect study in microbiology or medical education specifically.
- Brod's review does not give a pooled effect size for prediction. A recent pretesting/prequestion review (Pan & Carpenter, Educ Psychol Rev, 2023) was not retrieved this session.
