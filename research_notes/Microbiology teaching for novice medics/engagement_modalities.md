# Engagement-oriented teaching modalities for microbiology and infectious-disease education (novice Year 1 medical students; interactive web e-book)

Evidence-grading key used throughout:
- **[M]** = measured learning (test scores, pre/post knowledge, exam grades), even if single-site and uncontrolled
- **[MC]** = measured learning with a comparison or control group
- **[S]** = student satisfaction, perception or self-reported learning only
- **[D]** = description or design paper, opinion or commentary; no outcome data
- **[V]** = vendor or developer-reported; not independent

Overall picture: the microbiology-specific engagement literature is large but thin. Most papers are single-institution, small, uncontrolled, and report satisfaction **[S]**. Controlled learning outcomes **[MC]** are uncommon. Where they exist, they usually show an active modality to be **equal to or modestly better than** lectures or traditional preparation, not dramatically better.

## 1. Case-based learning (CBL) and PBL in microbiology for preclinical or novice students, including outbreak and epidemiological cases

### Takeaway
CBL and PBL in preclinical microbiology reliably raise engagement and attendance **[S]**. Their effect on measured knowledge is mixed: some studies find higher scores, others none. Published microbiology cases are almost always **diagnostic or clinical** (hypothesis → tests → treatment), which suits a foundation e-book poorly. Outbreak and historical-investigation cases (John Snow, pseudo-outbreaks) are a well-established way to teach transmission and the logic of microbial causation without clinical diagnosis or treatment. They rest on teaching resources rather than outcome studies.

### Cited Findings
**CBL/PBL outcomes in microbiology**
- **Vieira 2025, FEMS Microbiol Lett 372 (Federal Univ. Minas Gerais, Brazil)** **[S]**. PBL sat inside the basic-cycle microbiology course (3rd period of the medical course, students mostly 20–22 y). There were two case sessions per semester, one after bacteriology and one after mycology/virology, run in practical classes of 15–18 students. Step 1: students discussed a hypothetical case from prior knowledge only and formed hypotheses. Step 2: about 2 weeks later they returned with researched answers and proposed diagnostics, exams and initial treatment. Only 98 of about 360 students responded (27.2%). Results: 88.8% agreed the activity would help them retain content, 71.4% found it more motivating than seminars, and 79.6% wanted more PBL. Criticisms were large groups, **lack of guiding questions**, a setting less inclusive for introverts, and a wish for **a clear final resolution**. The author states the design could not objectively assess learning. — [Vieira 2025, FEMS Microbiol Lett, doi:10.1093/femsle/fnaf075](https://academic.oup.com/femsle/article/doi/10.1093/femsle/fnaf075/8217237)
- An Indian study (n=96) found CBL scores significantly higher than didactic-lecture scores (P<0.001), with the difference persisting at 4 weeks **[MC]**. Another prospective study of second-year students found **higher attendance (P=.008) but no difference in test performance (P=.98)** **[MC]**. A pilot CBL study reported 91% student satisfaction and 83% positive faculty response **[S]**. Most such studies are single-site and several are indexed only on ResearchGate or Academia.edu. — [Search summary of CBL studies incl. Impact of CBL in clinical microbiology on second-year students](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379149007_Impact_of_case-based_learning_approach_in_clinical_microbiology_on_second-year_students_A_prospective_observational_study); [CBL in a rural North Indian medical college](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jdrntruhs/fulltext/10.4103/jdrntruhs.jdrntruhs_201_20~case-based-learning-introducing-an-innovative-paradigm-for); [Pilot CBL study, students' perspective](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348998387_A_pilot_study_on_case-based_learning_CBL_in_medical_microbiology_students_perspective_httpsauthorselseviercomc1cWMn1LltrRJIz)
- A "Scenario-Case Discussion" model in medical microbiology produced a larger pre/post gain than controls (mean increase 1.99 vs 1.03 points, P<0.05) **[MC]**. — [PubMed 42454360](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42454360/)
- "An Interactive, Case-Based Microbiology Laboratory Session for First-Year Medical Students" reported median satisfaction of 4 or 5 on a 5-point scale across five years **[S]**. This is one of the few papers explicitly for Year 1. The full text could not be retrieved (PMC returned a CAPTCHA page). — [PMC13471905](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13471905/)
- In a large-group CBL vs lecture comparison for preclinical students (interstitial lung disease, not micro), CBL is an analogous preclinical model. — [PMC11272146](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11272146/)

**Outbreak and historical-investigation cases that teach transmission without diagnosis or treatment**
- A CDC classroom lesson (BAM! Body and Mind, now archived) uses John Snow, "considered to be the father of epidemiology," to ask how epidemiologists use scientific method to find causes of infectious disease **[D]**. — [CDC archive: epi_4_johnsnow.pdf](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/healthyschools/bam/teachers/documents/epi_4_johnsnow.pdf)
- The UNC NC Center for Public Health Preparedness overview of outbreak investigations uses Snow as a case. It notes that *Vibrio cholerae* was unknown to Snow, which separates epidemiological inference from lab identification. It also describes a Florida **pseudo-outbreak** in which *Cyclospora cayetanensis* was misidentified by the laboratory. Both are good novice cases about evidence and causation rather than clinical care **[D]**. — [UNC FOCUS: An Overview of Outbreak Investigations](https://nciph.sph.unc.edu/focus/vol1/issue1/1-1Overview_issue.pdf)
- A 2025 arXiv paper proposes recreating Snow's 1854 cholera map in R as a data-visualisation case. It suggests (a) a guided step-by-step replication and (b) an open-ended project with alternative visualisations **[D]**. — [Revisiting John Snow's Cholera Map (arXiv 2504.13970)](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13970v1)
- A College Board "Disease Outbreak Investigation" unit pairs a Snow cholera lesson with food-borne and environmental outbreak lessons, each with quizzes **[D]**. — [College Board: Disease Outbreak Investigation](https://secure-media.collegeboard.org/digitalServices/pdf/yes/disease_outbreak.pdf)
- A collection of infectious-disease field-epidemiology case studies exists as a teaching resource. — [Outbreak Investigations Around the World (PMC2854793)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2854793/)
- A teaching article presents an inquiry model based on Snow's life and work for biology lessons on the history and nature of science **[D]**. — [The Life and Work of John Snow](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234590759_The_Life_and_Work_of_John_Snow)
- A secondary-biology study built disease-transmission lessons on history-of-science stories and evaluated them with questionnaires, focus groups and observation, focusing on engagement and classroom climate **[S]**. — [Storytelling based on the history of science…](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357081984_STORYTELLING_BASED_ON_THE_HISTORY_OF_SCIENCE_AS_AN_EFFECTIVE_EDUCATIONAL_TOOL_-APPLICATION_IN_REAL_CLASSROOM_SETTINGS)

### Inferences
- Student complaints in Vieira 2025 (no guiding questions, no clear resolution) are exactly what a branching-MCQ e-book case can fix. Each decision node gives scaffolded prompts and feedback, and the case ends with an explicit resolution and "what this case taught".
- For a foundation block, re-scope cases from "what is the diagnosis and treatment?" to **"mechanism" or "investigation" questions**. Examples: *Why did this organism survive here? How did it get from A to B? What would you need to see under the microscope or on a plate to tell these two apart? Which of these interventions breaks the chain of transmission?* Snow (water-borne transmission, natural experiment), Typhoid Mary (asymptomatic carriage, the reservoir concept), the Cyclospora pseudo-outbreak (lab error, evidence quality) and a food-borne church-supper-style outbreak (attack rates, incubation period → organism class) all fit this template without needing pathology or pharmacology.
- CBL's most robust benefit is engagement and attendance. It is unlikely to be inferior for knowledge and is sometimes better. Pair cases with retrieval quizzes so measured learning is not left to chance.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed outcome study was found specifically on outbreak or epidemiological investigation cases (Snow, Typhoid Mary, the CDC Oswego church-supper case, "EpiCase"-style modules) for medical students. The resources exist, but evaluations were not found in this search.
- Full text of the Year-1 case-based lab paper (PMC13471905) and the Scenario-Case Discussion paper could not be retrieved; their case-design details are unverified.
- Not found: a study that directly compares "mechanism" cases with "diagnostic" cases for novices. That distinction is a design inference, not an evidenced finding.
- Existing systematic reviews of CBL in health professions (e.g., the BEME guide by Thistlethwaite et al., Med Teach 2012) were not re-retrieved in this session.

## 2. Narrative, storytelling and history-of-microbiology approaches

### Takeaway
Story-based approaches (comics, "micro-stories," history-of-science narratives) consistently increase interest and self-reported memorability **[S]**. Rigorous retention data are sparse. The best-known quantitative claim (stories → 21% better learning, about 48% better retention) comes from an unreviewed 2000 dissertation and is not microbiology-specific. Narrative is best treated as a hook and organising frame, not a stand-alone learning intervention.

### Cited Findings
- **JMBE (2025/2026), "The power of narrative storytelling: using comics to bring microbiology concepts to life" (UBC, 2nd-year microbiology)**. The authors note that narrative in microbiology teaching is understudied relative to its value in other fields and argue stories may support higher retention than text. Student comments say the comics' jokes and punchlines helped them remember Gram-positive vs Gram-negative differences **[S]**. — [JMBE doi:10.1128/jmbe.00113-26](https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/jmbe.00113-26); [PMC13520696](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520696/)
- "Storytelling and Cartoon Characters in Medical Microbiology: An Engaging Approach to Enhance Teaching and Learning" is a medical-microbiology example of personified organism characters **[S/D]**. — [ResearchGate 387520639](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387520639_Storytelling_and_Cartoon_Characters_in_Medical_Microbiology_An_Engaging_Approach_to_Enhance_Teaching_and_Learning)
- "Leveraging Micro-Stories to Build Engagement, Inclusion, and Neural Networking in Immunology Education" uses short narratives inside lectures in immunology, an adjacent field **[D/S]**. — [PMC6893969](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6893969/)
- "Storytelling as a Tool to Enhance Conceptual Knowledge in Cell Biology" (JMBE) is the adjacent cell-biology example, the domain these students have just studied. — [JMBE doi:10.1128/jmbe.00308-21](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jmbe.00308-21)
- A 2000 PhD dissertation reports students taught through stories learned concepts about 21% better and retained close to 48% more than with traditional lessons. It links narrative structure to the history of science, since a narrative's spatiotemporal structure mirrors the unfolding of discovery **[MC, but unreviewed and not microbiology]**. — [The value of storytelling in the science classroom (ADS abstract)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2000PhDT........16I/abstract)
- A review of storytelling in biology classrooms notes that most storytelling studies are qualitative and do not show a clear relationship to learning and retention. — [The Utility of Storytelling Strategies in the Biology Classroom](https://www.cedtech.net/download/the-utility-of-storytelling-strategies-in-the-biology-classroom-6210.pdf)
- History-of-science stories on disease transmission were used in secondary biology classes, evaluated qualitatively for engagement **[S]**. — [ResearchGate 357081984](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357081984_STORYTELLING_BASED_ON_THE_HISTORY_OF_SCIENCE_AS_AN_EFFECTIVE_EDUCATIONAL_TOOL_-APPLICATION_IN_REAL_CLASSROOM_SETTINGS)
- Pasteur himself conceived immunity as largely passive. The active-defence framing came later and is propagated via school history stories such as Jenner's work. This is a reminder that history narratives carry framings with them. — [Aeon: Why we should guard against military notions of immunity](https://aeon.co/essays/why-we-should-guard-against-military-notions-of-immunity)

### Inferences
- In the e-book, use history (Snow → water-borne transmission; Semmelweis → hand hygiene and contact transmission; Koch → postulates and causation; Pasteur → spontaneous generation and sterilisation; Lister → antisepsis; Fleming → microbial antagonism, with no pharmacology needed) as **chapter-opening problem narratives**. Each one poses the question the chapter answers ("How could Semmelweis prove what was killing the mothers without knowing about bacteria?"). Each should end in a retrieval check so the story does more than entertain.
- First-person "microbe's-eye view" narratives fit organism-diversity chapters (a niche, survival strategy and transmission route told by the organism). They need explicit "where the metaphor breaks" boxes, because microbes are not goal-directed (see Section 3 on anthropomorphism).
- An LLM-simulated chat with a *historical figure* (e.g., "interview Semmelweis" or "ask John Snow about the pump") is a novel adaptation. It suits Year 1 because it needs no clinical reasoning. There is no evidence base specific to it; it would need factual guardrails.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found measuring the retention effect of history-of-microbiology narratives (Semmelweis, Koch, Pasteur, Fleming, Lister) in medical students.
- No evaluated "microbe biography" or first-person microbe narrative intervention was found beyond the comics and cartoon-character papers.

## 3. Analogies and metaphors in microbiology teaching, and the risks of misleading metaphors

### Takeaway
Analogies are "double-edged swords". They aid understanding when mapped explicitly and their limits are stated, as in Glynn's Teaching-With-Analogies (TWA) model. They seed misconceptions when students over-map, including anthropomorphism and goal-directed reasoning about microbes. War and military metaphors for infection and immunity are criticised in medicine, anthropology and microbiome science. Evidence that they cause harm in learners is mixed and largely argumentative, not experimental.

### Cited Findings
- Glynn's guidance on making science concepts meaningful sets out the TWA approach: introduce the target, cue the analog, identify shared features, map them, **indicate where the analogy breaks down**, and draw conclusions **[D]**. — [Glynn 2008, Making science concepts meaningful to students](http://osu-wams-blogs-uploads.s3.amazonaws.com/blogs.dir/548/files/2010/10/Glynn2008MakingScienceConceptsMeaningful.pdf)
- Advances in Physiology Education "Personal View": analogies are double-edged. Analogy-induced misconceptions arise either from an inadequate source domain or from students reducing the target concept to only what was mapped from the source. The recommended remedy is asking focused questions about features *not* shared **[D]**. — [Analogies in science and science teaching, Adv Physiol Educ, doi:10.1152/advan.00022.2010](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/advan.00022.2010)
- CBE–LSE study of a graduate-TA course on analogies: students can overgeneralise analogy similarities to the point of misconception. One example is assuming bacteria have different genders from a mating analogy for conjugation. Anthropomorphic framings such as a protein as a "shoplifter" encourage goal-directed reasoning **[M/D]**. — [CBE—Life Sci Educ, doi:10.1187/cbe.22-07-0142](https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.22-07-0142)
- A study of analogies in a ninth-grade biology textbook analysed how analogies are presented (mapping, limits stated or not) **[D]**. — [Dikmenli, APFSLT](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v16_issue1/dikmenli/page4.htm)
- NABT lesson (Am Biol Teacher, Sep 2024) uses a robber / superhero / "most wanted" poster analogy for vaccines. It cites an Austrian survey in which only 29% of freshman biology students agreed vaccination against some viral diseases is possible, which illustrates entering misconceptions **[D]**. — [NABT ABT Online Sep 2024](https://nabt.org/files/galleries/ABT_Online_Sep_2024.pdf)
- **War-metaphor critiques.** A National Academies (IOM) workshop summary, "Ending the War Metaphor: The Changing Agenda for Unraveling the Host-Microbe Relationship," argues the war metaphor is a limiting mental shortcut. The point is critical for microbiome/commensal framing. — [NCBI Bookshelf NBK57060](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK57060/)
- *Medical Anthropology Theory*, "Beyond the military metaphor": military metaphors collapse diverse interests into "fronts," obscure alternative responses and promote human exceptionalism, though they communicate urgency. — [MAT](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/index.php/mat/article/view/5026)
- Science-education commentary lists the standard war-analogy elements in popular immunology texts (invading enemy, body as fort, defending army) and argues they misrepresent immunity. — [science-education-research.com](https://science-education-research.com/disease-and-immunity-a-biological-myth/)
- Isaacs 2019, J Paediatr Child Health: battle and war metaphors used with children with cancer may cause harm **[D]**. — [doi:10.1111/jpc.14411](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpc.14411)
- **Counter-evidence**: analysis of COVID-19 political communication challenges earlier claims that the war metaphor is inherently negative and damaging. — [PMC8022596](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8022596/)
- Pasteur conceived immunity as passive; the defence metaphor was built later. — [Aeon](https://aeon.co/essays/why-we-should-guard-against-military-notions-of-immunity)

### Inferences
- E-book convention: each analogy component (Gram envelope as "walls and moat," virus as "hijacker/program," quorum sensing as "voting/headcount," spores as "survival pods," plasmids as "USB sticks") should carry a **TWA-structured "maps / doesn't map"** panel. Add one MCQ that targets the non-shared feature, e.g. "Does the 'hijacker' virus *intend* to replicate?" or "Does conjugation involve sexes?".
- The book is about diversity and microbes rather than immunity, and most microbes are commensal or environmental. So default to **ecological framings** (niches, competition, cooperation, colonisation, transmission routes) and reserve conflict language for specific cases. This also prepares students for general pathology (host–microbe balance) without front-loading immunology.
- Avoid teleological phrasing ("bacteria want to…", "the virus tries to…") in narrative components, or flag it explicitly.

### Gaps
- No controlled trial was found testing a specific microbiology analogy (e.g., Gram envelope) against non-analogical instruction.
- No empirical study was found measuring whether war metaphors in microbiology/immunology teaching change medical students' conceptions. The critique is mainly theoretical.

## 4. Educational games in microbiology and infectious disease (card and board games, escape rooms, gamified quizzes, serious and commercial games)

### Takeaway
Many microbiology games are published, mostly in JMBE: Bacttle, Immune Battle, MicroBioCombat, KROBS, student-designed games, Plague Inc. labs, escape rooms. Engagement **[S]** is near-universally positive. A few show pre/post knowledge gains **[M]**. Gamified retrieval (Kahoot, gamified question banks) has the most consistent association with exam scores, though it is correlational or quasi-experimental. No microbiology-specific systematic review of games was found.

### Cited Findings
**Card and board games**
- **Bacttle** (JMBE 2024): a microbiology board game in which players take a bacterial species. It uses a card deck, a 3D-printed board (paper alternative) and tokens, and is fully open source. With 169 science-fair visitors, pre/post surveys showed knowledge gains, largest in players with **no prior microbiology** (ages as young as 5) **[M, uncontrolled]**. The paper also lists other serious games: Outbreak!, Biofilm building, MedMyst. — [JMBE doi:10.1128/jmbe.00097-24](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jmbe.00097-24); [PubMed 39082784](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39082784)
- **Immune Battle** (JMBE 2024): a board game for introductory microbiology and immunology. It improved student experience and engagement with difficult material, and students requested more such activities **[S]**. — [JMBE doi:10.1128/jmbe.00075-24](https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/jmbe.00075-24)
- **MicroBioCombat**: a Magic: The Gathering-inspired card game on bacterial pathologies, transmission vectors and prophylaxis; design and classroom use are described **[D/S]**. — [PMC13450638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13450638/)
- **KROBS** (card game of 20 microbes; transmission modes): at a medical-resident "game evening," residents' scores on microbiology/ID evaluations rose significantly from initial to summative **[M, uncontrolled]**. — [JMBE doi:10.1128/jmbe.00119-21](https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/jmbe.00119-21)
- **Student-driven game design** (JMBE 2021): students designed games in a virtual classroom, e.g. a biofilm-formation board game and a fast drawing game for microbiology review **[D/S]**. — [JMBE doi:10.1128/jmbe.v22i1.2323](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jmbe.v22i1.2323)
- A general review of card and board games in medical education ("More than mere games") is not microbiology-specific. — [ResearchGate 5690087](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5690087_More_than_mere_games_A_review_of_card_and_board_games_for_medical_education)

**Escape rooms**
- An ID-themed escape room for medical students and residents (OFID 2026) had eight objectives (isolation precautions, lab safety, MRSA-active antimicrobials, etc.). All 25 survey respondents agreed it was engaging, fun and enjoyable, and most reported improved understanding and confidence **[S]**. The pre/post knowledge test was still ongoing at the abstract stage. — [OFID ofag561](https://academic.oup.com/ofid/article/13/9/ofag561/8786178); [abstract PMC12792277](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12792277/)
- An interdisciplinary escape room in German undergraduate medicine (psychiatry + ID + communication; one deadly infectious disease plus infection prevention and control) used pre/post knowledge questionnaires **[M]**. — [BMC Med Educ, doi:10.1186/s12909-025-07990-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-025-07990-2)
- A 2026 interprofessional escape room (Med Sci Educ) had positive ratings, but the authors caution about a small control group **[MC, weak]**. — [Med Sci Educ, doi:10.1007/s40670-026-02661-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40670-026-02661-3)
- An escape room on Gram-positive antimicrobial resistance for pharmacy students (IDWeek 2018) produced mean correct scores of 90.5% pre-class, 82.1% post-case and 90.5% post-escape room. That is no net gain over baseline **[M]**. — [OFID ofy210.1144](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/ofid/ofy210.1144)
- An antimicrobial-stewardship escape room for PharmD students was mapped to educational outcomes. — [PMC10677337](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10677337/)

**Gamified quizzes**
- Gamified supplemental microbiology question bank (2022): higher class exam scores were associated with greater use **[M, correlational]**. — [PubMed 35531347](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35531347/)
- Kahoot in pathology (Helwan, Egypt; 55 vs 55, retrospective quasi-experimental): Kahoot use was significantly associated with better pathology performance (P=0.001) but not with general academic performance **[MC, quasi]**. — [DOAJ, J Adv Med Educ Prof 2022](https://doaj.org/article/190fd527bbd142c6b9eeb440d83f4087)
- Kahoot in first-year histology for dental and medical students (Eastern Finland; 215 students; compared with a prior cohort): mostly positive ratings and suggested benefits **[S/M]**. — [Dovepress AMEP](https://www.dovepress.com/feasibility-of-kahoot-as-a-real-time-assessment-tool-in-histo-patholog-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-AMEP)
- Kahoot field study (200 bachelor students, 14 weeks): more quiz participation was associated with higher exam scores **[M, correlational]**. — [Periodica Polytechnica](https://pp.bme.hu/so/article/view/12464)

**Serious and commercial games**
- **Plague Inc.**: the Harvard Active Learning Labs "Plague Inc. Immunology Lab" has students read an epidemiology handout, play, and map game choices to agent, host and environment factors in transmission **[D]**. — [Harvard ABLConnect](https://ablconnect.harvard.edu/book/plague-inc-immunology-lab)
- A college microbiology instructor (Delta College) has used Plague Inc. since 2013 to reinforce microbial genetics, epidemiology and pathogenesis. This is anecdotal and posted on the developer's site **[V/D]**. — [Ndemic Creations guest blog](https://www.ndemiccreations.com/en/news/88-guest-blog-plague-inc-educating-through-infection)
- In a cross-sectional study of the Taiwanese public, people who had played Plague Inc. had higher COVID-19 knowledge and attitudes. This is an association only **[correlational]**. — [PMC8025917](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8025917/)
- A Brazilian paper says the game suits teaching from late middle school through university, but notes it was designed to entertain and its simplifications limit accuracy **[D]**. — [ResearchGate 374149358](https://researchgate.net/publication/374149358_ASSESSMENT_OF_THE_POTENTIAL_APPLICATION_OF_THE_GAME_PLAGUE_INC_AS_A_PLAYFUL_TOOL_FOR_TEACHING_SCIENCE_AND_BIOLOGY)

### Inferences
- The most evidence-aligned "game" elements for an e-book are **gamified retrieval practice**: streaks, points and timed rounds on quizzes and flashcards. The proposed mechanism is retrieval and spacing, not fun per se.
- Card and board games can be ported to Svelte components. Examples: an organism "trump card" deck comparing size, envelope type, oxygen requirement, genome type and transmission route (a non-catalogue way to explore diversity), and a transmission-route "build the chain" puzzle.
- Escape-room logic (locked puzzles needing microscopy, Gram, growth-condition or transmission reasoning to unlock) works as a chapter-capstone component without any clinical content. Evidence is satisfaction-level only.
- A Plague-Inc-style "design a pathogen / break the chain" simulation (Section 5) can teach the trade-offs between transmissibility, environmental survival and host range. Design it to avoid that game's known inaccuracies, and frame it as an epidemiology tool rather than an "evil pathogen" game.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluations were found for games named in the brief: "Microbe Wars," "Bug Wars," "Pathogen" board game, "Infection Investigation". These may exist under other titles or not be published.
- Microbe Mission is a Science Olympiad event (US schools). No education-outcome literature for it was found.
- No microbiology-specific systematic review or meta-analysis of games was found. A general medical-education games review exists but was not retrieved in full.

## 5. Interactive simulations and virtual labs

### Takeaway
The best independent evidence (PLOS ONE, Labster microbiology pre-lab) shows virtual labs are **as effective as** face-to-face tutorials for lab preparation, knowledge, motivation and self-efficacy, not superior. Larger effect claims are vendor-reported. Students prefer hybrid virtual-plus-physical. Simulations make most sense in an e-book where a real lab is impossible or unsafe, or as pre-lab rehearsal.

### Cited Findings
- **Makransky et al. 2016, PLOS ONE**: a Labster simulation (isolating a food-poisoning bacterium) used as preparation for a physical microbiology lab performed the same as face-to-face tutorials on lab scores. Both groups gained similarly in knowledge, motivation and self-efficacy **[MC]**. — [PLOS ONE doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0155895](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0155895)
- Tripepi (Thomas Jefferson Univ., JMBE 2022) studied a semester microbiology lab curriculum with Labster in remote and in-person formats. Labster's press release claims a 19% test-score improvement over reading materials; this figure was not verified against the paper **[V]**. — [Labster news](https://www.labster.com/news/research-higher-test-scores)
- Labster's evidence page lists institutional figures (e.g., Yavapai College gateway micro course completion 70%→87%) **[V]**. — [Labster evidence](https://www.labster.com/guides/evidence-labster-virtual-labs-work)
- A veterinary immunology Labster implementation (Frontiers Vet Sci 2025): students found it useful, and the authors state virtual simulations are not a complete substitute for hands-on labs **[S]**. — [PMC12411196](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12411196/)
- NC A&T college microbiology (MDPI 2024): 57.2% of students favoured hybrid in-person plus virtual labs **[S]**. — [MDPI 2813-4346/3/4/49](https://www.mdpi.com/2813-4346/3/4/49)
- Plague Inc. Immunology Lab (above) is an example of a game-based transmission simulation mapping agent, host and environment. — [Harvard ABLConnect](https://ablconnect.harvard.edu/book/plague-inc-immunology-lab)

### Inferences
- The e-book's Svelte simulations (growth curve with adjustable temperature, pH and O2; a Gram stain step simulator where students choose decolourisation time and see over- or under-decolourisation artefacts; serial dilution and CFU counting; an SIR/agent-based transmission model with sliders for R0, contact rate, environmental persistence and asymptomatic carriage; a 16S/phylogeny tree builder for diversity) should be **predict → run → explain** tasks. Prediction prompts give a simulation its learning value beyond watching an animation.
- Because "equivalent to tutorials" is the best-evidenced outcome, position simulations as **preparation for, or a reinforcement of,** whatever wet lab or practical the NUS block contains, not a replacement.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed outcome studies were found in this search for specific virtual Gram-stain simulators, growth-curve simulators, agent-based epidemic models or phylogenetic tree builders in medical students. Tools such as HHMI BioInteractive epidemiology interactives and NetLogo epidemic models were not evaluated here.
- The Tripepi JMBE 2022 primary data were not retrieved.

## 6. Visual approaches: microscopy galleries, 3D and scale visualisations, visual taxonomies, and visual mnemonics (Sketchy, Picmonic)

### Takeaway
Commercial visual-mnemonic resources (Sketchy Micro, Picmonic) are widely used for board-exam recall. There is **no independent head-to-head or rigorous controlled evidence** for their effect on learning in MD students. Their effectiveness claims are vendor-generated. Critiques centre on equity (cost), misalignment with curricula and a factoid-recall orientation. Peer-reviewed evaluations of conceptual visuals (scale visualisations, microbial "zoo" taxonomies) in medical microbiology were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Picmonic claims it is "proven to improve long-term memory retention by up to 331%" and helps students score 50% higher; the source study is not shown **[V]**. — [Picmonic microbiology](https://www.picmonic.com/microbiology); [Picmonic vs Sketchy comparison page](https://www.picmonic.com/picmonic-alternatives/compare-sketchy-medical-vs-picmonic)
- Sketchy's blog says users report high retention months to years later, but cites no data **[V]**. — [Sketchy blog](https://www.sketchy.com/blog/why-is-sketchy-micro-and-pharm-so-effective)
- A quasi-experimental pilot of an audiovisual mnemonic resource (Picmonic) in a podiatric medical ID course (2 cohorts, 158 second-year students, access vs control) is the only controlled study surfaced. Its results were not retrieved **[MC, unverified]**. — [JMIR Med Educ, PMC11835597](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11835597/)
- "Responding to a new generation of proprietary study resources in medical education" (J Med Libr Assoc) argues these third-party resources can widen the achievement gap between students who can and cannot pay, and discusses how institutions should respond **[D]**. — [PMC6466489](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6466489/)
- Third-party commentary: Picmonic targets concise recall; Sketchy offers a more in-depth story-sketch experience **[D, non-academic]**. — [TheMDJourney](https://themdjourney.com/picmonic-vs-sketchy/)
- Student comics feedback says visual jokes aided recall of the Gram-positive/negative distinction **[S]**. — [JMBE comics paper](https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/jmbe.00113-26)

### Inferences
- Sketchy and Picmonic encode *organism → disease → drug* facts. That is exactly the systems-level, pharmacology-dependent catalogue this block avoids, so they are a poor model for Year 1 foundation content. The e-book's visuals should instead encode **concepts and comparisons**. Examples: a logarithmic size-scale zoom (virus → bacterium → fungus/protist → human cell, linking to the cell biology students already know); an interactive Gram-envelope cross-section; a microscopy gallery organised by *structural feature* (shape, arrangement, stain reaction, spores, capsules) rather than by organism name; a visual "tree of life" or "microbial zoo" where students sort organisms by envelope, genome or oxygen requirement.
- If mnemonic images are used, use them sparingly, for foundational distinctions only, and pair them with explanatory mechanism.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation was found of size-scale visualisations, 3D models or microbial-zoo-style visual taxonomies in medical microbiology.
- The Picmonic podiatry pilot's outcome data were not retrieved.
- No independent study was found evaluating Sketchy Micro on learning in MD students in this search.

## 7. Team-based learning (TBL) and flipped classroom in medical microbiology

### Takeaway
In basic-science laboratory and adjacent courses, flipped classroom plus TBL (FC-TBL) produces modestly higher test scores than flipped-only or lecture-based learning **[MC]**, with strong preference for its engagement. Microbiology-specific TBL evidence is thin. One pipeline-course paper reports improved learning of microbiology concepts (title only seen).

### Cited Findings
- Zhejiang basic medical laboratory course (n=408, 3rd-year): FC-TBL beat flipped-only on immediate post-tests and on final exams in 2 of 3 experiment sessions. Students preferred FC-TBL for engagement, feedback and learning environment **[MC]**. — [BMC Med Educ 2022, doi:10.1186/s12909-022-03676-1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-022-03676-1)
- Molecular-biology lab FC-TBL vs lecture-based learning (mixed methods, 2024): scores 90.27±3.51 vs 85.89±4.26, with higher scores in some later related courses. One course (Hospital Infection, 8th semester) favoured the lecture group **[MC]**. — [BMC Med Educ 2024, doi:10.1186/s12909-024-06450-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-024-06450-7)
- Blended FC-TBL in a public-healthcare education course improved knowledge, problem-solving and satisfaction vs lectures **[MC]**. — [PMC7845047](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7845047/)
- "Team-Based Learning in a Pipeline Course in Medical Microbiology for Under-Represented Student Populations in Medicine Improves Learning of Microbiology Concepts". The title claims improved learning; details were not retrieved. — [ResearchGate 312549373](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312549373_Team-Based_Learning_in_a_Pipeline_Course_in_Medical_Microbiology_for_Under-Represented_Student_Populations_in_Medicine_Improves_Learning_of_Microbiology_Concepts)

### Inferences
- The e-book is the natural **pre-class (flipped) layer**. TBL's structure is individual readiness test (iRAT) → team test (tRAT) → application exercise. That maps directly onto e-book chapter quizzes as the iRAT, with class time for team application problems such as outbreak cases.
- Expose quiz analytics to faculty (if feasible) so in-class time targets weak items.

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled study of TBL specifically in medical microbiology for Year 1 was retrieved. Systematic reviews of TBL in health professions (e.g., Reimschisel et al., Med Teach 2017) were not re-retrieved in this session.

## 8. Student interest and motivation in microbiology and ID as a career, and approaches shown to raise interest

### Takeaway
ID faces a sustained recruitment problem in the US. In 2020, 86 of 406 ID fellowship positions went unfilled. A brief 2020 "Fauci effect" was followed by renewed decline, with only 330 applicants for the 2023 match. Most residents who choose ID became interested before or during medical school. Cervantes and colleagues argue that memorisation-heavy microbiology teaching deters interest, and that case-based or experiential (research) exposure is associated with more interest. That evidence is self-report from small samples.

### Cited Findings
- **Cervantes J. "The Future of Infectious Diseases Education." Med Sci Educ 2020**: describes a multi-year decline in internal-medicine residents applying to ID; 86 of 406 ID fellowships were unfilled in the 2020 NRMP match. Most uninterested residents had initially considered ID, and salary, lack of procedures and training length were the main deterrents. The article argues medical education can help reverse this **[D]**. — [Med Sci Educ, doi:10.1007/s40670-020-01023-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40670-020-01023-x); [PMC7358297](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7358297/)
- **Cervantes & Hong 2021, JMBE 22:e00101-21**: 14 of 16 students who completed a mandatory host-pathogen research project at TTUHSC El Paso, 2017–2019, were surveyed. 85.8% said it increased interest in microbiology and immunology, 92.9% reported more confidence, 92.8% found microbiology and immunology relevant, interesting and enjoyable, and 64.3% expected their career to involve micro/immunology. There was no control and no pre-measure, and the data are retrospective self-report **[S]**. The authors cite prior work that most medical learners rely on memorisation and that students exposed to case-based or non-memorisation learning are more likely to choose ID. They note almost three-quarters of residents who chose ID became interested before or during medical school. — [PMC8439612](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8439612/)
- Clinical Infectious Diseases 2014, "Infectious Diseases Subspecialty: Declining Demand Challenges and Opportunities," is an IDSA-linked analysis of declining demand. — [CID 59(11):1593](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/11/1593/1746396)
- Fill-rate analysis of the 2019–2021 match cycles. — [J Infect Dis, doi:10.1093/infdis/jiab549](https://doi.org/10.1093/infdis/jiab549)
- The 2020 "Fauci effect" produced a record match rate. Applicants then fell to 330 for the 2023 match, and early 2026 data show continued decline. — [STAT News 2022](https://www.statnews.com/2022/12/07/infectious-disease-fellowship-drop-in-applicants/); [NPR 2022](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2022/12/12/1142250941/newest-doctors-shun-infectious-diseases-specialty); [Today's Hospitalist](https://todayshospitalist.com/infectious-disease-fellows-drop-female-residents-take-majority/)
- COVID-19's impact on trainee career decisions. — [PMC8600172](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8600172)

### Inferences
- For a Year 1 e-book, the interest levers with some support are: (a) reducing pure memorisation in favour of reasoning (cases, mechanism questions); (b) authentic science (stories of how things were discovered, real data such as Snow's map and outbreak curves, research-flavoured "you are the investigator" tasks); and (c) relevance hooks (current outbreaks, AMR as a teaser for later pharmacology, the microbiome). Short "who does this work?" career vignettes (clinical microbiologist, ID physician, epidemiologist, public-health lab scientist; NUS/Singapore examples such as NCID) are a low-cost addition, though their effect is not evidenced.
- The US recruitment data do not transfer directly to Singapore. The underlying "memorisation deters interest" argument is general.

### Gaps
- No Singapore- or Asia-specific data on medical students' interest in microbiology or ID careers was found.
- No controlled study was found showing a preclinical microbiology teaching intervention changed ID career choice. Existing evidence is self-reported interest.
- The original studies linking case-based exposure to choosing ID (cited within Cervantes & Hong) were not retrieved.
