# Curriculum-level alternatives to the "bug parade" for teaching general microbiology to first-year medical students

Scope note: These notes cover organising frameworks, threshold concepts, concept inventories, medical-school course examples, spiral design, integration with pathology and immunology, and choosing exemplars. Evidence across this literature is mostly **descriptive or consensus-based**. I found **no controlled study** comparing a concept-first and a taxonomy-first general microbiology course for novice medical students. Several PMC/ASM full texts were blocked (captcha or 403), so a few items rest on abstracts and repository records, as flagged below.

## 1. What organising frameworks have been proposed or used instead of taxonomy-first catalogues?

### Takeaway
Two expert consensus frameworks directly support a pathogen-free "foundational" layer organised by concepts:
- the 2026 AMSMIC medical objectives (64 foundational objectives that name no specific pathogens, then 125 systems objectives);
- the 2024 ASM undergraduate guidelines (29 fundamental statements across 6 concept areas: evolution, cell structure/function, metabolism, information flow, microbial ecology, impact of microbes).

Mechanism- and strategy-based frameworks fit a foundation block well:
- "pathogen lifestyles" and common virulence strategies;
- the Pirofski–Casadevall damage-response framework (DRF), which began as a teaching tool.

These frameworks are proposals and consensus documents. None has outcome data for medical novices.

### Cited Findings

**AMSMIC 2026 (Bauler, Arvidson et al.)**
- Citation: Bauler TJ, Arvidson CG, Akbar S, Arvidson DN, Bauer ME, Baxa DM, Greenblatt RJ, Honer zu Bentrup K, Liu Y, Phoenix I, Kerry JA. *J Microbiol Biol Educ* 2026;27(2):e00042-26, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00042-26. Bauler and Arvidson contributed equally. The work began at the AMSMIC Educational Strategies Workshop. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/); [JMBE](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jmbe.00042-26)
- Method: a hybrid of Nominal Group Technique and Delphi. It started from 679 content areas in the AMSMIC 2009 and IDSA 2010 documents. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- Foundational objectives (64) deliberately name no specific pathogens. They are organised as:
  - Bacteriology: structure, metabolism, genetics
  - Virology: structure and replication, genetics, pathogenesis
  - Mycology and parasitology
  - Disease processes: microbial pathogenesis and host–pathogen interactions
  - Diagnosis, treatment and prevention

  Source: [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- Systems objectives (125) are grouped into six organ systems (cardiovascular, GI, genitourinary, nervous, respiratory, skin/musculoskeletal), plus blood/systemic and zoonotic/opportunistic. They are framed around presentations. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- Guiding principles:
  - organise around clinical disease presentation;
  - focus on common pathogens, while being "inclusive of rarer pathogens associated with severe disease";
  - reflect current identification methods;
  - stay flexible for emerging and regional pathogens.

  Source: [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- On the bug parade: standalone courses "often take a traditional 'bug parade' approach," which the authors say can weaken clinical recall and interest in infectious disease. Medical microbiology "must move beyond the 'bug parade'" and its reliance on memorising isolated facts. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- Evidence base: expert consensus, not outcome data. Most objectives map to lower-order Bloom's levels. Summary objectives are meant to prompt case-based, higher-order application. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)

**ASM Curriculum Guidelines 2024 (Boury et al.)**
- Citation: Boury N, Siegesmund A, Kushner DB, Smyth DS, Allen ME, Frazier A, Gillette-Ferguson I, Markum M, Patriquin G, Reynolds SE, Rosario S, Steel JJ, Horak R. *J Microbiol Biol Educ* 25(3), Dec 2024, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00126-24, PMC11636387. It updates the 2012 guidelines and extends the language to eukaryotic microbes, viruses and other acellular agents. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1128/jmbe.00126-24&resultType=core&format=json); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f98cb23d34a142469691f79abda0c380)
- The 2024 structure has 29 fundamental statements (FS) in 6 areas. "Microbial Systems" was renamed "Microbial Ecology". Statements most relevant to a medical foundation block:
  - **Evolution.** FS2: diversity arises through gene transfer, mutation, reassortment, recombination and natural selection.
  - **Cell structure and function.**
    - FS6: distinct microbial structures are targets for antimicrobials and host immunity.
    - FS7: structures are adapted to functions that confer fitness.
    - FS9: virus replication depends on structure, genome type and host cells.
    - FS11 (new): obligate intracellular microbes require living host cells.
  - **Metabolic pathways.**
    - FS12: metabolic diversity.
    - FS13 and FS14: intrinsic and extrinsic factors affect survival and growth.
  - **Information flow.**
    - FS17: information flow differs among domains.
    - FS19: acellular agents depend on host processes.
  - **Microbial ecology.**
    - FS22: most microbes are beneficial or neutral; a minority are detrimental.
    - FS23 (new): One Health.
  - **Impact of microbes.**
    - FS28 (new): a minority of microbes are pathogens.
    - FS29 (new): microbial damage can be limited by host and external factors, including the microbiome, antibiotics and immunity.

  Source: [Boury et al. 2024 full text via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11636387/fullTextXML)
- The 2012 version had 27 fundamental statements, 4 scientific thinking skills and 7 lab skills. In 2024 the skills were combined into 8. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f98cb23d34a142469691f79abda0c380); [Boury 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11636387/fullTextXML)
- ASM presents the guidelines as recommendations, not a mandate. — [ASM guidelines page](https://asm.org/guideline/asm-curriculum-guidelines-for-undergraduate-microb); [ASM news Aug 2024](https://asm.org/articles/2024/august/new-asm-curriculum-guidelines-foster-microbial-lit)
- Companion virology learning objectives were published in the same issue: Kushner et al., doi:10.1128/jmbe.00100-24. — [Duquesne record](https://dsc.duq.edu/faculty/1525)

**Damage-response framework (Pirofski and Casadevall)**
- The DRF was devised in 1999 while the authors were preparing to teach a microbial pathogenesis course, so it began as a teaching tool. It was formally proposed in 2003 (*Nat Rev Microbiol*, doi:10.1038/nrmicro732).
- It is neither microbe-centred nor host-centred. The outcome of infection is set by the amount of host damage, which can come from microbial factors and/or the host response.
- It is "frequently used ... to teach microbial pathogenesis to diverse learners."

  Source: Nosanchuk JD, mGem, *mBio* 2025;16(3):e0294524, doi:10.1128/mbio.02945-24 — [PMC11898690](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11898690/); [Einstein record](https://einstein.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/mgem-a-quarter-century-with-the-pirofskicasadevall-damage-respons/)
- ASM popular framing: "Pathogenesis is not a trait—it's an outcome." — [ASM 2024](https://asm.org/articles/2024/june/pathogenesis-not-a-trait-its-an-outcome)
- The DRF was used to organise COVID-19 manifestations (Pirofski and Casadevall 2020, doi:10.1128/mbio.01175-20). — [DOAJ mBio 2020](https://doaj.org/article/489831b564b945739fa55130e4c1b470)

**Pathogen "lifestyle" and strategy-based course designs (course catalogues; no outcome data)**
- University of Kentucky, Mechanisms of Microbial Pathogenesis: organised around pathogen lifestyles. It uses selected pathogens that illustrate particular lifestyles and pathogenic strategies, rather than full coverage. — [UKY](https://www.as.uky.edu/node/1047377)
- University of Surrey, Pathogenesis of Infectious Diseases: organised by mechanism, including toxin-mediated disease, secretion systems, opportunistic disease, and metabolism as a pathogenic strategy. — [Surrey](https://catalogue.surrey.ac.uk/2019-0/module/MMIM024)
- Indiana University BIOL-M 541: organised around general strategies for colonising a host and around host defence. — [IU](https://academics.iu.edu/courses/bloomington/biol-m-541-microbial-pathogenesis-and-virology.html)
- Universitat de València, Microbial pathogenesis: organised around adhesion, colonisation, invasion, biofilm, toxins, secretion systems and immune evasion. — [UV](https://www.uv.es/recursos/fatwirepub/guias/33153-ANG-2026-27.pdf)

**The HPI concept set as a framework**
- The Host–Pathogen Interactions Concept Inventory (HPI-CI) defines 13 concepts. These include:
  - "diverse microbes share common strategies for interacting with hosts";
  - "microbes use various strategies to cause disease";
  - pathogens and hosts co-evolve;
  - pathogens differ from non-pathogens;
  - the environment affects a bacterium's pathogenic phenotype.

  Taken together, the set is a strategy-based framework. — [Marbach-Ad et al. 2009, PMC3577151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577151/)

**Older calls to replace the bug parade**
- An IAMSE commentary argued the bug parade could easily be replaced by case-based activities and simulations. — [IAMSE](https://www.iamse.org/mse-article/the-effectiveness-of-the-lecture/)
- Mims' Medical Microbiology describes itself as systems-based, placing the "bug parade" in clinical context. This is publisher framing, not evidence. — [Elsevier](https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/mims-medical-microbiology-9780702072017.html)

### Inferences
- AMSMIC's foundational/systems split matches the NUS sequence almost exactly: foundation = pathogen-free concepts; organ blocks = presentation-organised systems objectives. The e-book can map chapters to the AMSMIC foundational headings and use the ASM 2024 statements as the "big ideas" spine.
- ASM FS6, FS11, FS22, FS28 and FS29 together give a ready-made narrative:
  - most microbes are harmless;
  - pathogenicity is a minority outcome;
  - structure determines both fitness and vulnerability (to immunity and drugs);
  - obligate intracellularity is a lifestyle;
  - damage is limited by host factors.

  This is close to a DRF framing.
- A "how a microbe makes a living" lens is well represented in graduate pathogenesis courses (Kentucky, Surrey, IU, Valencia) but undocumented for medical novices. Adapting it would be a reasonable but untested design choice.

### Gaps
- No controlled or comparative outcome study of concept- or strategy-organised versus organism-organised general microbiology for medical novices was found.
- The DRF's parabolic curve classes (Classes 1–6) were not verified in a fetched source this session. Consult Casadevall and Pirofski 2003 (doi:10.1038/nrmicro732) directly.
- No published study was found that uses Koch's postulates or molecular Koch's postulates as an organising lens for a medical course. This needs separate searching (e.g. Falkow 1988).
- Full text of Boury 2024 was reached only via the Europe PMC XML. Fundamental statements above are paraphrased by the fetch tool, not quoted.

## 2. Threshold concepts in microbiology

### Takeaway
The Meyer and Land threshold-concept framework is rarely applied to microbiology or medical microbiology. Only small, descriptive projects and outcome-statement documents exist. No empirical study was found that identifies threshold concepts in infection or microbiology for medical students.

### Cited Findings
- **Neve et al. (2016)** argue the threshold-concept framework is rarely applied in medical education and suggest how it could inform curriculum design. — [Plymouth PEARL](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/8594)
- **Burke et al. (2016), "Threshold learning outcomes for a microbiology major"** (*Microbiology Australia*, MA16031). This is an Australian outcomes statement, not an empirical threshold-concept study. — [Unimelb record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1279888-threshold-learning-outcomes-for-a-microbiology-major); [Macquarie](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/threshold-learning-outcomes-for-a-microbiology-major/)
- **University of Leicester (Came and Heaphy, 2018 poster)**, Year 1 Biological Sciences microbiology:
  - A student partner wrote 20 TopHat audience-response questions targeting "challenging threshold concepts", drawing on peers' early misconceptions.
  - The example shown is metabolic classification: chemotrophy, heterotrophy, phototrophy, autotrophy, chemolithotrophy.
  - The questions were to be piloted in 2018/19; no outcomes were reported.

  Source: [Leicester DIP poster](https://dip.le.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Poster_Came-1.pdf)
- A 2024 systematic review of threshold concepts in natural-science teaching found they are not always addressed well in curricula or teaching methods, and called for research on effective strategies. — [Review record](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/doaj_a22eb638536f42bc92e8646f7412645e/Description)

### Inferences
- Concept-inventory data (Section 3) give an empirical proxy for "troublesome knowledge" in microbiology. Candidate threshold concepts for novices:
  - selection acts on phenotype (antibiotic resistance);
  - metabolic diversity and energy classification;
  - prokaryote–eukaryote differences in information flow;
  - viral dependence on host cells;
  - pathogenicity as an outcome of host–microbe interaction, not a fixed trait (DRF).

  These are the author's inference, not findings of a threshold-concept study.

### Gaps
- No study of threshold concepts in medical microbiology or infection was found (e.g. no Delphi or interview study with medical students).
- The Cox and Simpson (2018 JMBE) paper on microbiology education and infection-control competency was seen only in a reference list. Whether it uses threshold concepts is unverified. — [reference list](https://www.ee.ucl.ac.uk/mflanaga/CountryListAU.html)

## 3. Microbiology concept inventories: what students struggle with

### Takeaway
Two validated inventories (HPI-CI 2009; MCI 2017) agree on where students struggle:
- metabolism (lowest scores and no learning gains, even after instruction);
- prokaryote vs eukaryote differences, including information flow;
- viral replication's dependence on host cells;
- vaccine and genome-manipulation concepts;
- selection acting on phenotype rather than genotype (antibiotic resistance).

These are exactly the "foundational" topics a bug parade skips.

### Cited Findings

**HPI-CI (Marbach-Ad et al. 2009)**
- Citation: Marbach-Ad G, Briken V, El-Sayed NM, et al. *J Microbiol Biol Educ* 2009;10(1):43–50, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v10.98.
- Format: a two-tier instrument (multiple choice plus a written explanation).
- The 13 concepts:
  1. structure affects pathogenicity
  2. common strategies across diverse microbes
  3. natural selection drives virulence and resistance
  4. gene-expression changes in response to environment
  5. various disease strategies
  6. host–pathogen co-evolution
  7. cell wall and membrane affect responses
  8. pathogens vs non-pathogens
  9. environment affects pathogenic phenotype
  10. metabolic adaptation
  11. self vs non-self
  12. innate vs adaptive, general vs specific recognition
  13. immune memory is specific

  Source: [PMC3577151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577151/)
- HPI-CI findings:
  - Metabolism (concept 10): Q13 scored 9–27% correct pre-instruction and 9–26% post. No high-scoring students answered it correctly, which revealed a curriculum gap.
  - Gram stain (Q2): as low as 3% correct pre-instruction in one General Microbiology class.
  - Antibiotic resistance (Q1), General Microbiology (n=68): 38 students said selection acts on genotype alone, 25 said "both", and 1 said phenotype. 28 students did not understand that selection acts on phenotypes.

  Source: [PMC3577151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577151/)
- Scale: the HPI-CI has been given pre/post to about 400 students per year since 2006. — [UMD CBMG](https://cbmg.umd.edu/research/special-initiatives/assessment-and-concept-inventory/)
- Stevens, Smith, Marbach-Ad et al. (2017 JMBE) used the HPI-CI before an introductory microbiology course. Patterns of antibiotic-resistance answer choices were highly correlated across institutions, which implies shared misconceptions. — [DOAJ 2017](https://doaj.org/article/6a3203f4342c4b76af586e7503688783); [VTechWorks](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/ef38fff7-5216-4b20-b288-e7cdf95484d5)
- A further paper presents the CI as a tool for faculty professional development (*CBE—Life Sci Educ*). — [LSE](https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.10-05-0069)

**Microbiology Concept Inventory (MCI; Paustian et al. 2017, JMBE)**
- Built by faculty from 11 institutions on the ASM 2012 fundamental statements. MCI 3.0 has 23 items covering all 27 statements. Reliability: Cronbach's α 0.705; Ferguson's δ 0.97. — [PMC5976036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976036); [Iowa State](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/d62b294f-f380-4887-8964-71113704e522)
- Pre/post at 10 institutions showed gains everywhere except metabolism (normalised gain 0.15). — [PMC5976036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976036)
- Lowest post-test item difficulties (n=1,161; proportion correct):

  | Item | Topic | Post-test |
  |---|---|---|
  | Q11 | metabolic diversity | 0.24 (no gain) |
  | Q19 | genome manipulation / vaccine function | 0.25 |
  | Q17 | viral replication depends on host cells | 0.27 |
  | Q16 | information-flow differences among Bacteria, Archaea and Eukaryotes | 0.29 |
  | Q9 | prokaryote vs eukaryote cell properties | 0.36 |

  Mean difficulty across the inventory was about 0.55. — [PMC5976036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976036)
- Average post-test difficulty by ASM area:

  | Area | Average post-test |
  |---|---|
  | Evolution | ~0.59 |
  | Cell structure | ~0.49 |
  | Metabolism | ~0.45 |
  | Information flow | ~0.45 |

  Source: [PMC5976036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976036)
- A companion Microbiology for Health Sciences Concept Inventory (MHSCI, 23 items) was also reported as reliable and discriminating. — [DOAJ JMBE 2017](https://doaj.org/article/a1a4f59421f54c85ba3e41c48ef948d4)

### Inferences
- For an e-book: oxygen and energy metabolism (aerobe/anaerobe) and the virus-as-obligate-parasite idea are where novices fail most and gain least. They deserve interactive, conceptual treatment (e.g. "what does this organism need to grow?"), not a table.
- The phenotype/genotype selection misconception is directly relevant to the later AMR and pharmacology blocks. It is worth seeding in foundation even though drug details are deferred.
- These inventories were validated on undergraduate biology and microbiology students, not medical students. Transfer to NUS Year 1 is plausible but unverified.

### Gaps
- The Briggs et al. 2017 companion paper listing MCI item-level misconceptions was not retrieved.
- No concept inventory validated specifically on medical students was found.

## 4. Medical-school examples of restructured microbiology, and outcome data

### Takeaway
US medical schools have moved heavily toward integration: 45% had fully integrated microbiology content by 2016. Course directors report fewer hours and integration difficulties. Published redesigns are almost all descriptive. They report student satisfaction or feasibility, not controlled learning outcomes.

### Cited Findings

**Surveys of US schools**
- Melber DJ, Teherani A, Schwartz BS. "A comprehensive survey of preclinical microbiology curricula among US medical schools." *Clin Infect Dis* 2016;63(2):164–168, doi:10.1093/cid/ciw262, PMID 27126343.
  - Survey of course directors and curriculum deans at 142 schools; 104 responded.
  - Challenges reported: integrating microbiology with other courses, and reduced hours.

  Source: [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC11000598%20OR%20PMCID:PMC11898690%20OR%20DOI:10.1093/cid/ciw262%20OR%20DOI:10.1128/jmbe.v10.i1.97&resultType=core&format=json)
- AMSMIC 2026 cites Melber: "45% of medical schools had fully integrated medical microbiology content." It also notes preclerkship phases shrinking to 12–18 months and pass/fail Step 1. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- A companion CID commentary: course leaders struggle to weave microbiology into other parts of the curriculum, cover content in fewer hours, and balance basic and clinical science. — [CID 63(2) issue](https://academic.oup.com/cid/issue/63/2)
- Bauer ME, Akbar S, Bauler TJ, Chacon J, McClelland EE, Staudaher S, Zhao Y. "Exploration of the integration of microbiology and immunology emerging topics into undergraduate medical education." *Med Educ Online* 2024;29(1):2336331, doi:10.1080/10872981.2024.2336331.
  - Faculty survey on adding emerging topics (COVID-19, Zika, mRNA vaccines, mpox).
  - Barriers include a crowded curriculum.

  Source: [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC11000598%20OR%20PMCID:PMC11898690%20OR%20DOI:10.1093/cid/ciw262%20OR%20DOI:10.1128/jmbe.v10.i1.97&resultType=core&format=json); [IU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/items/37d1a76d-98db-4731-8a32-1289e3625be9)

**IDSA guidance**
- Southwick F et al., IDSA Preclinical Curriculum Committee, 2010 commentary in *Academic Medicine* (not CID).
  - It criticises reliance on PowerPoint lectures and multiple-choice tests.
  - It proposes just-in-time teaching, peer instruction in teams of four, edited textbooks and internet sources, small-group discussion of pathogenesis and differential diagnosis, and essay questions.
  - It also proposes a national consensus on factual content to reduce information overload.

  Sources: [Einstein record](https://einstein.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/commentary-idsa-guidelines-for-improving-the-teaching-of-preclini-3/); [UF Health news 2010](https://news.drgator.ufl.edu/2010/02/23/infectious-disease-experts-propose-more-effective-way-to-teach-the-topic/)

**Steinel et al. 2019, Dell Medical School**
- Citation: "Integration of Microbiology, Pharmacology, Immunology, and Infectious Disease Using Active Teaching and Self-Directed Learning." *Med Sci Educ* 29(1):315–324, PMC8368113.
- Format: 6–7 weeks, about 12 contact hours per week, flipped (podcasts, self-directed time, team-based learning, simulation, PILLARS small-group cases).
- Organisation, 2016–17: by organism class (week 1 Gram-positive bacteria; week 2 Gram-negative; week 3 viruses; week 4 helminths and protozoa), then week 5 clinical ID by syndrome and site. Exemplars in each class were "chosen for how prevalent or serious the infection is."
- Organisation, 2017–18: ID content spread throughout. Epidemiology and transmission concepts were introduced in week 1, and Gram-positives were expanded to a full week.
- Outcomes: no comparative outcome data. Step 1 data are "not shown". Only 39.2% of students had prior microbiology or immunology.

  Source: [PMC8368113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8368113/)

**Other descriptive examples**
- University of Kansas: an optional case-based wet lab for first-year medical students (small groups solve short bacterial and fungal cases). Three consecutive years; well received by 57 students. Satisfaction only. — [PMC12792493](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12792493/)
- Indiana University: TBL built on real case reports, deliberately limited to eight prevalent pathogens. — [IMPRS](https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/IMPRS/article/view/24517)
- Saarland University: a web-based course built on clinical scenarios, theory and technique videos, with a retrospective before-and-after comparison against the on-site course. Results not extracted this session. — [PMC10012015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10012015/)
- India: PBL for microbiology with 159 medical students; perception data. — [PMC5837329](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5837329/)
- Colombo, Sri Lanka (Perera, Ameen, Rajakaruna): an organ- and system-based approach to *foundation* microbiology, with student feedback. — [Univ. Colombo](https://www.res.cmb.ac.lk/medicine/jennifer-perera/pubs/an-organ-system-based-approach-to-teaching-foundation-microbiology/)
- GermBugs (UK): story-based microbiology teaching for undergraduate medics. *Microbiology Today* 2022. — [White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/187048)
- Course split example: one programme puts about 30% of microbiology in introductory material on bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites, with the rest taught by organ system. — [search summary; programme roadmaps e.g. Usask](https://medicine.usask.ca/documents/ugme/roadmaps/2024-2025/microbiology-2024-2025.pdf)
- IAMSE: "Design and Implementation of Core Knowledge Objectives for Medical Microbiology and Immunology" (*Medical Science Educator*). Not retrieved. — [IAMSE](https://www.iamse.org/mse-article/design-and-implementation-of-core-knowledge-objectives-for-medical-microbiology-and-immunology/)

### Inferences
- Even "integrated" exemplar courses such as Steinel's often keep an organism-class skeleton (Gram+, Gram−, viruses, parasites). It looks like the dominant default, not a rejected one. The innovation is in pedagogy (flipped, TBL, cases) and exemplar curation, not in abandoning taxonomy.
- The strongest published evidence is satisfaction and feasibility. Claims that concept-first teaching improves learning are therefore expert opinion.

### Gaps
- Melber 2016's detailed results (hours, organism- vs system-based percentages) could not be retrieved; PubMed and CID pages were blocked.
- No prematriculation microbiology course with outcome data was found in this session.
- No Singapore or Asian medical-school microbiology curriculum study was found beyond the Indian PBL and Sri Lankan examples.

## 5. Spiral curriculum applied to microbiology (foundation → systems)

### Takeaway
The spiral curriculum is widely invoked (Bruner; Harden) and many schools structure microbiology as a short general introduction revisited in organ-system blocks. No study was found that tests a microbiology-specific spiral. The best pre-clerkship evidence concerns retrieval-based revisiting of earlier block content.

### Cited Findings
- A pre-clerkship study framed organ-system blocks as vulnerable to forgetting. It cites estimated retention of about 70% at 1 year, 40–50% at 2 years and about 30% at 4+ years.
  - Intervention: a formal spiral of 500 board-style MCQs delivered in mandatory 10–20-question sessions revisiting prior blocks (class of 2022).
  - Students were receptive but preferred formats that didn't use personal time.

  Source: [PMC9848358](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9848358)
- Whole-curriculum spiral examples:
  - Alfaisal: structure and function revisited in later disease-mechanism modules. — [Alfaisal](https://com.alfaisal.edu/en/mbbs)
  - Dow University: system-related modules, with an infectious-disease module. — [DUHS ID module guide](https://www1.duhs.edu.pk/curriculum/downloads/INFECTIOUS%20DISEASE%20MODULE%20STUDY%20GUIDE%202023-050723.pdf)
- AMSMIC 2026 does not discuss spiralling explicitly. It says preclerkship is one step, reinforced in clerkship, post-clerkship and residency. Its foundational objectives can be embedded in systems courses. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)

### Inferences
- A natural NUS spiral:
  - **Turn 1 (foundation):** organism-agnostic concepts (structure → fitness and vulnerability; energy and oxygen; obligate intracellularity; the viral replication cycle; transmission; the damage-response outcome).
  - **Turn 2 (organ blocks):** the same concepts recur with named pathogens, sites, diagnosis and treatment.
  - **Turn 3 (pharmacology):** structure-as-target (ASM FS6) and resistance via selection.

  The e-book can plant explicit "you will meet this again in…" hooks and retrieval questions.

### Gaps
- No published microbiology-specific spiral design with outcome data was found.

## 6. Integration with general pathology and immunology at foundation level

### Takeaway
The supporting frameworks all treat pathogenesis as a joint host–microbe outcome, which naturally bridges general pathology (tissue injury, inflammation) and immunology:
- the DRF (damage from microbe and/or host response);
- ASM FS29 (damage limited by microbiome, antimicrobials and immunity);
- the HPI-CI's three immunology concepts.

AMSMIC explicitly acknowledges overlap with immunology, pathology and pharmacology, and recommends collaborative teaching. No foundation-level integration study with outcomes was found.

### Cited Findings
- AMSMIC: "some learning objectives may overlap with immunology, pharmacology, or pathology." The authors suggest collaborative teaching in integrated curricula. Pharmacology is handled by separate objectives. — [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- In the DRF, damage can come from microbial factors or the host immune response. The framework is used to teach pathogenesis to diverse learners. — [PMC11898690](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11898690/)
- ASM 2024 statements:
  - FS6: microbial structures are targets for host immunity and antimicrobials.
  - FS29: microbial damage is limited by host and external factors, including the microbiome and immunity.

  Source: [Boury 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11636387/fullTextXML)
- The HPI-CI deliberately mixes microbiology and immunology concepts (11–13: self/non-self; innate vs adaptive; specific memory). Its authors treat the two as one conceptual domain. — [PMC3577151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577151/)
- MCI: immunology is not one of the six ASM areas and was added as an extra statement (Q10, difficulty 0.54). — [PMC5976036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976036)
- Steinel 2019 co-taught microbiology, immunology, pharmacology and ID as one unit, mostly team-taught. — [PMC8368113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8368113/)

### Inferences
- The DRF lets the e-book connect to general pathology just taught (cell injury, acute and chronic inflammation, granulomas) by framing disease as host damage. Examples: toxin-mediated damage (microbe-driven) vs immunopathology (host-driven).
- It also previews immunology by pairing each microbial strategy (capsule, intracellular niche, antigenic variation) with the defence it counters.
- These are design inferences, not tested findings.

### Gaps
- No published foundation-level module integrating general pathology (inflammation) and microbiology, with evaluation, was found.

## 7. Conveying organism diversity with a limited set of exemplars

### Takeaway
Published practice selects exemplars by **clinical prevalence and severity**. This is used in AMSMIC (common pathogens plus rarer severe ones), Steinel (exemplars per class chosen for prevalence and seriousness) and Indiana (eight prevalent pathogens). Strategy-based pathogenesis courses select exemplars that **illustrate a lifestyle or strategy**. No study was found that compares exemplar numbers or selection criteria, or that tests a "prototype organism, then contrast" design in medical education.

### Cited Findings
- AMSMIC: focus on common pathogens and include rarer pathogens associated with severe disease.
  - Rarer causes are "intentionally excluded" unless severe.
  - Organisms that rarely change management may be omitted.
  - A consolidated pathogen list is in Appendix 3 (supplement; not retrieved).
  - Schools should add regional and emerging pathogens.

  Source: [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)
- Steinel 2019: each organism class represented by exemplars "chosen for how prevalent or serious the infection is in clinical medicine." — [PMC8368113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8368113/)
- Indiana University TBL: eight prevalent pathogens a physician is more likely to encounter. — [IMPRS](https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/IMPRS/article/view/24517)
- University of Kentucky: selected pathogens that illustrate particular lifestyles and pathogenic strategies. — [UKY](https://www.as.uky.edu/node/1047377)
- ASM FS22 and FS28 stress that pathogens are a minority of microbes. FS25: microbes serve as models for fundamental life processes. Both support model-organism framing (e.g. *E. coli*). — [Boury 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11636387/fullTextXML)

### Inferences
- A defensible exemplar strategy for a foundation e-book is a small "diversity matrix". Each cell is a contrastive axis taught through one well-chosen exemplar:
  - Gram+ vs Gram−
  - aerobe vs anaerobe
  - extracellular vs facultative vs obligate intracellular
  - DNA vs RNA virus; enveloped vs naked
  - yeast vs mould
  - protozoan vs helminth

  Exemplars would be chosen for both clinical prevalence (per AMSMIC and Steinel) and how cleanly they illustrate the axis (per Kentucky).
- A local (Singapore and SE Asia) prevalence filter would fit AMSMIC's call for regional flexibility.
- The exact number of exemplars has no evidence base. Cognitive-load reasoning would favour roughly one per axis, with later organ blocks adding the rest.

### Gaps
- No empirical work on the optimal number of exemplars, or on "prototype then contrast" (comparative or analogical) designs in microbiology education, was found. A follow-up could look in the analogical-encoding and contrasting-cases literature (e.g. Gentner; Schwartz and Bransford), which was outside this session.
- The AMSMIC Appendix 3 pathogen list was not retrieved.
