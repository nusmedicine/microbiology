# Misconceptions about microbes, infection and antimicrobials, and ecological / microbiome / One Health framings, for a foundation-level medical microbiology e-book (NUS Year 1)

Method note: Searches ran through WebSearch and the Europe PMC REST API (abstracts and open-access full text). PubMed Central pages were blocked by a captcha, so the open-access full texts of Briggs 2017, Richard 2017 and Pickett 2022 were read through Europe PMC instead. Where a figure comes only from a search-engine summary and not a source I read myself, it is marked "(secondary / verify)".

## 1. Documented misconceptions: prevalence and populations

### Takeaway
The best-quantified misconceptions concern antibiotic resistance. About 57–76% of laypeople and students think the *body* or *human*, not the bacterium, becomes resistant. About 81% of undergraduates think bacteria mutate "in order to" survive. Belief that antibiotics work on viruses or colds is held by 64% of the public across 12 countries, and by roughly 30–40% of medical and health students in pooled reviews. Concept-inventory work adds misconceptions that matter for a foundation book: resistance needs prior exposure (43%), vaccines must cause disease (22%), all bacteria need oxygen (20%), and prokaryotes have mitochondria. Several misconceptions in the brief (fungi as plants, parasites only in the tropics, fever caused directly by the microbe, sterile body sites) have little or no prevalence data that I could find.

### Cited Findings

**Public: WHO multi-country survey (2015)**
- 9,772 respondents in 12 countries, two per WHO region (Barbados, China, Egypt, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Nigeria, Russia, Serbia, South Africa, Sudan, Viet Nam), fieldwork Sep–Oct 2015. WHO notes the results are not representative of regions or the world. — [WHO news release, 16 Nov 2015](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance)
- 64% believed antibiotics treat colds and flu. 32% believed you should stop antibiotics when you feel better. **76% thought the body, not the bacteria, becomes resistant.** 44% thought resistance affects only people who take antibiotics regularly. 66% thought they were not at risk if they took antibiotics as prescribed. 57% felt there was little they could do. 64% believed medical experts would solve the problem. 73% said farmers should give fewer antibiotics to food animals. — [WHO 2015](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance)
- Asian country data: China 61% believed antibiotics treat colds/flu, and 53% would stop when feeling better. India 75% believed antibiotics treat colds/flu. Indonesia 63% believed antibiotics treat colds/flu. **Viet Nam 86% thought the body becomes resistant (highest of all countries).** — [WHO 2015](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance)

**Singapore public**
- Lim JM, Duong MC, Cook AR, Hsu LY, Tam CC. Online survey of 706 Singapore residents, May–June 2019. **62.5% thought resistance occurs when "our bodies become resistant to antibiotics"**, and 48.5% thought it happens when antibiotics "become less powerful". Better knowledge was associated with more favourable attitudes, and favourable attitudes with lower odds of expecting or receiving antibiotics from a primary care doctor. — [BMJ Open 2021;11:e048157, doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2020-048157](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2020-048157)
- A 2023 survey of 1,002 Singapore adults found that adherence to antibiotics was driven mainly by the perceived cost of seeing a doctor, then by seeing resistance as a threat, and only to a lesser extent by knowledge. — [BMC Public Health 2023 (DOAJ record)](https://doaj.org/article/75947f92270749d98b9c3f4837960a74) (secondary / verify)
- An Annals Academy of Medicine Singapore letter (2025) on private primary care notes that most acute respiratory infections are viral and self-limiting, so antibiotics are usually unnecessary. This is the local clinical context the misconception feeds into. — [Ann Acad Med Singap 2025;54(4):260–263](https://annals.edu.sg/acute-respiratory-tract-infections-management-in-private-primary-healthcare-in-singapore/)

**Medical and health-science students (including Asia)**
- Systematic review (PRISMA) of 20 studies in 13 countries covering 13,997 medical students: **39.5% considered antibiotics effective against viral infections**, 35.9% had self-medicated with antibiotics, and 49.1% had recently prescribed them. — [Revista de Salud Pública / Univ. del Rosario](https://revistas.urosario.edu.co/index.php/revsalud/article/view/13449) (secondary / verify)
- Auta et al. pooled analysis of healthcare students, 131 studies in 43 countries: 70.0% knew antibiotics are ineffective against viruses, but **only 57.9% (95% CI 51.5–64.3) knew they are ineffective against colds and flu**. The conference abstract (86 studies) reported lower correct knowledge in Thailand (30.3%) and Turkey (35.8%). — [Auta et al., Br J Clin Pharmacol 2026 (repository)](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/59045/); [Auta et al. abstract P21, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12258457/) (secondary / verify)
- East China, medical students at one university, 1,066 respondents 2017–2022: AMR knowledge rose over time, but 30.2–45.2% said there is no risk of AMR as long as antimicrobials are taken correctly. Gaps remained on antimicrobial targets and bacterial transmission. — [Front Public Health 2022, doi:10.3389/fpubh.2022.1010582](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.1010582/full)
- Brunei undergraduates (Antibiotics 2020): **76% wrongly believed resistance results from the body becoming resistant.** Note that versions disagree on sample size (n = 130 vs 145). — [Antibiotics 2020, PMC7344439](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7344439/)
- Malaysia (2014), medical and pharmacy students: only 15.4% recognised *S. pyogenes* as not penicillin-resistant, and 27.6% recognised Enterococcus as vancomycin-resistant. — [J Infect Dev Ctries (jidc.org)](https://jidc.org/index.php/journal/article/download/24916878/1090/31093) (secondary / verify; attribution from search summary)
- Nigeria, healthcare students, pre/post education: good knowledge rose from 28.1% to 44.6%, but resistance misconceptions persisted (24.9% → 17.2%, p = 0.055, not significant). — [Springer Nature "Behind the paper"](https://communities.springernature.com/amp/posts/impact-of-educational-intervention-on-knowledge-of-antimicrobial-resistance-and-antibiotic-use-patterns-among-healthcare-students-a-pre-and-post-intervention-study) (secondary / verify)
- I found no Singapore study of medical students' microbiology or AMR misconceptions (see Gaps).

**Undergraduate biology: intuitive reasoning about resistance (most rigorous data)**
- Richard, Coley & Tanner 2017. Non-majors (n = 58), entering majors (n = 319), advanced majors (n = 107) and faculty (n = 14) at a large urban US university. Agreement with three misconception statements:
  - **Teleological** ("bacteria develop mutations to become resistant and survive"): **81% of students** (advanced majors still 74%).
  - **Anthropocentric** (resistance develops because of changes within humans): **57%** (entering majors 64%), versus 0% of faculty.
  - **Essentialist** (bacteria are genetically alike and equally likely to be killed): 37%, versus 7% of faculty.
  - 73% of students produced inaccurate information in open explanations, and "the body becomes resistant" was among the most common ideas.
  - 91% agreed resistance is an example of evolution, yet only 10–28% of students mentioned natural selection (faculty 71%).
  - Agreeing with a misconception was significantly associated with using the matching intuitive reasoning (all p ≤ 0.05).
  — [CBE Life Sci Educ 2017;16:ar55, doi:10.1187/cbe.16-11-0317](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.16-11-0317)
- Instructors use construal-consistent (anthropic, teleological, essentialist) language in every class sampled: 90 classes across 18 courses at two universities. Anthropic language was the most frequent. — [Betz et al., CBE Life Sci Educ 2019;18:ar63, doi:10.1187/cbe.19-04-0076](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.19-04-0076)

**Concept inventories**
- **Microbiology Concept Inventory (MCI) development:** 743 students at 8 US institutions before an introductory microbiology course (mostly biology/microbiology majors, one allied-health section). 19 misconception themes each appeared in at least 5% of coded answers. The top five, as a share of coded explanations:
  1. Bacteria must be exposed to an antibiotic before they can mutate to resistance (**43%**).
  2. Vaccines must cause disease to work (**21.7%**).
  3. Oxygen is required for, or speeds up, bacterial growth (**19.5%**), with "fermentation requires oxygen" as a related error.
  4. Similar gene sequences come only from vertical descent, ignoring horizontal gene transfer (**19.4%**).
  5. Confusion of RNA, RNA polymerase and primase (**19%**).
  Other themes, without percentages: prokaryotes have organelles such as mitochondria; all prokaryotes respond alike to penicillin; adaptive immunity is conflated with drug resistance; mutations are always harmful.
  — [Briggs et al., J Microbiol Biol Educ 2017;18(3), doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319)
- The validated 23-item MCI (Cronbach's α = 0.705) showed an average normalised learning gain of only 0.15. Metabolism items had the weakest gains. — [Paustian et al., JMBE 2017, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1320](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1320)
- Five years of MCI data at a rural US university: scores differed by GPA and favoured white students, which the authors read as possible ethnic bias. Relevant if the inventory is used outside the US. — [Rennpferd et al., JMBE 2023, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00110-22](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00110-22)
- **Host-Pathogen Interaction Concept Inventory (HPI-CI):** a two-tier instrument (answer plus written justification) covering 13 HPI concepts, given pre and post to about 400 students a year at the University of Maryland from 2006. — [Marbach-Ad et al., JMBE 2009](https://doaj.org/article/926899db5e5a45ddad24734ba95ec4ea)
- On one HPI-CI antibiotic-resistance item, distractor frequencies were highly correlated between two research universities, suggesting shared misconceptions across institutions. — [Stevens et al., JMBE 2017;18(1), doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i1.1281](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i1.1281)
- AMR misconceptions were "widespread among undergraduates". Inquiry-based and community-based learning both improved conceptual understanding. — [Schutz et al., JMBE 2026, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00217-25](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00217-25)

**"All microbes are harmful / germs" (children and secondary pupils)**
- Byrne studied 414 children aged 7, 11 and 14 in England. Two dominant themes appeared across all ages: anthropocentric ideas, and the view that microorganisms are essentially malevolent. The negative view shaped thinking about classification, living things, disease and decay. Children associated microbes with dirt and danger, and the focus on harm "may inhibit future learning". — [Byrne 2011, Int J Sci Educ 33(14):1927–61, doi:10.1080/09500693.2010.536999](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2010.536999); [Byrne thesis, Southampton](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/466291)
- Indonesian high school students mostly perceived microorganisms as harmful. Brazilian secondary pupils mainly saw microbes as disease-causing germs, and a laboratory sequence improved their understanding. — [Biosfer (UNJ)](https://journal.unj.ac.id/unj/index.php/biosfer/article/download/65737/26255); [EduCAPES (Brazil)](https://educapes.capes.gov.br/bitstream/capes/704991/1/SECONDARY.pdf) (secondary / verify)
- "Germaphobia" (fear of and aversion to dirt and microbes) was linked to lower nature connectedness and lower microbial literacy in an online survey (preprint, not peer-reviewed). — [Robinson et al., bioRxiv 2021](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.08.430200.full.pdf)

**Viruses as living cells or as small bacteria**
- In Spanish pre-service primary teachers, the most widespread misconception was counting viruses as living beings, not acellular agents. Antibiotic misconceptions also appeared. — [Univ. Salamanca record](https://produccioncientifica.usal.es/documentos/5da998352999527bba180997?lang=en)
- In pre-nursing students who had already taken college biology, there were systematic misconceptions about how viruses differ from bacteria. Thirty students who said viruses are alive were tested after interventions. Teaching the basic nature of viruses fixed the classification, but wrong *inferences* about viruses persisted. — [Univ. Arizona dissertation](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/290010)
- Indonesian high school three-tier test: mean misconception rate 47.2%. The highest (56.8%) concerned the discovery of viruses and how to distinguish viruses from bacteria. — [J. Pendas (Unpas) 2024](https://journal.unpas.ac.id/index.php/pendas/article/view/63305)

**Size and scale**
- Students tend to treat everything too small to see as roughly the same size, lumping micro- and nanoscale objects together without benchmarks, and often confuse the relative sizes of atoms and cells. (Tretter, Jones, Andre, Negishi & Minogue 2006, cited in a Purdue lesson plan.) — [Purdue "How Small is Nano" lesson](https://www.physics.purdue.edu/psas/docs/How%20Small%20is%20Nano%20Lesson.pdf) (secondary; original J Res Sci Teach 2006 not read)
- 3D-printed bacterial cell models gave statistically significant gains in learning objectives and in confidence. — [Wollmuth et al., JMBE 2023, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00089-23](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00089-23)

**Hand hygiene (medical students)**
- 523 Indian medical undergraduates on a WHO-adapted questionnaire:
  - About 7% had good knowledge.
  - About 69% did not know the correct order of handwashing and hand-rubbing.
  - 71.9% routinely used alcohol hand rub, but only 36.1% knew the contact time needed.
  - Only 27% identified the patient's own flora as the most frequent source of healthcare-associated infection. This is a direct "endogenous infection" / microbiota misconception.
  - About 57% had no formal hand-hygiene training.
  — [Modi et al., Cureus 2017 (PMID 28936375)](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/28936375)
- Final-year South African medical students (n = 107): mean score 46.8%, and only 10.5% knew hands should not be rinsed after alcohol rub. — [S Afr Fam Pract 2018, doi:10.4102/safp.v60i3.4883](https://dx.doi.org/10.4102/safp.v60i3.4883)
- In an Indian medical vs nursing comparison, 35.9% of medical students (vs 8% of nursing students) wrongly believed alcohol rubs dry the skin more than handwashing. — [IJCMPH](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/2510/1874/10034) (secondary / verify; attribution from search summary)

**Infection ≠ disease (host-dependence)**
- The outcome of a host–microbe interaction cannot be predicted from host or microbe alone; it has emergent properties. A host is defined as an entity housing a microbiome, and outcomes range from symbiosis, colonisation, commensalism and latency to disease. — [Casadevall & Pirofski, Infect Immun 2015;83:2–7, doi:10.1128/iai.02627-14](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.02627-14)
- Eleven attributes explain why individuals exposed to the same microbe have different outcomes, given as the mnemonic **MISTEACHING**: Microbiome, Inoculum, Sex, Temperature, Environment, Age, Chance, History, Immunity, Nutrition, Genetics. — [Casadevall & Pirofski, Infect Immun 2018;86:e00636-17, doi:10.1128/iai.00636-17](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.00636-17)
- HFMD in Singapore: 17.1% of childcare-centre children (n = 245) had enterovirus in saliva with no HFMD symptoms when sampled. This is local evidence that infection does not equal disease. — [Singapore HFMD cohort 2013–2018, PMC7901731](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7901731/)
- Nipah, Singapore 1999: only about 12 of 22 seropositive abattoir workers (about 54%) were symptomatic. — [Epidemiol Infect, Nipah risk-group survey](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/145043E616F1ED495CEA6D49A2E07AA9/S0950268801006422a.pdf/a-survey-of-nipah-virus-infection-among-various-risk-groups-in-singapore.pdf)

### Inferences
- The resistance cluster is the highest-yield target. "My body becomes resistant" (anthropocentric), "bacteria mutate in order to survive" (teleological) and "bacteria must be exposed first" (a Lamarckian / induced-mutation view) look like one underlying model: change is driven by need or exposure, not by selection on pre-existing variation. A single refutation-text unit plus a selection simulation could target all three. The Singapore public figure (62.5%) and the Viet Nam figure (86%) make it locally relevant.
- Year 1 NUS medical students will be academically strong, but the US advanced-majors data (74% teleological agreement) suggest prior biology study does not remove intuitive reasoning. Assume these misconceptions are present.
- "Vaccines must cause disease" and "adaptive immunity = drug resistance" are worth pre-empting even though immunology comes later. A short refutation box ("resistance belongs to the microbe; immunity belongs to the host") would separate the two ideas before they get conflated.
- Hand-hygiene data showing that only about 27% know patients' own flora are the main source of healthcare-associated infection is a natural bridge into microbiota teaching: endogenous infection = commensal in the wrong place or wrong host state.
- Betz 2019 matters for e-book authors: the book's own prose should avoid "bacteria want to / try to / learn to resist".

### Gaps
- No prevalence data found for: fungi as plants; parasites only in the tropics; fever or inflammation caused directly by the microbe rather than the host response; "body sites are sterile" (beyond the hand-hygiene item above). These are likely real but were not quantified in the sources I located.
- No Singapore or NUS study of medical students' microbiology misconceptions was found. A pre-course diagnostic (MCI or HPI-CI items) could fill this and would itself be publishable.
- Several student-survey figures (39.5% review, Auta 2026, Malaysia 2014, Nigeria) were taken from abstracts or search summaries; full texts not read.
- Tretter et al. 2006 (scale) original not read; size data are thin for microbiology specifically.

## 2. Conceptual-change approaches: refutation texts, cognitive conflict, POE, and evidence in biology and microbiology

### Takeaway
Refutation texts have the strongest evidence. A pre-registered meta-analysis (Danielson et al. 2025; 76 studies, 294 effect sizes) found a consistent advantage over standard text, and an earlier meta-analysis reports g ≈ 0.41. In microbiology, a JMBE classroom study (Pickett et al. 2022) found refutation readings cut agreement with the teleological resistance misconception from about 59% to 14%. A single reading did not change how students explained the idea in their own words, so refutation should be paired with active tasks. Evidence for predict-observe-explain (POE) in undergraduate microbiology is essentially absent; it comes from school-level studies with weak designs.

### Cited Findings
- **Danielson et al. 2025**, pre-registered meta-analysis. 71 articles (53 published, 18 unpublished), 76 studies, 111 samples, 294 effect sizes. It found a "consistent and statistically significant advantage" of refutation over non-refutation texts in controlled experiments, and none of 26 moderators meaningfully changed the effect. — [Educ Psychol 2025;60(1):23–47, doi:10.1080/00461520.2024.2365628](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2024.2365628)
- Earlier random-effects meta-analysis: 44 comparisons, n = 3,869, moderate effect **g = 0.41** (p < .001) versus other learning conditions, consistent across contexts. I believe this is Schroeder & Kucera 2022, *Educ Psychol Rev*, but I took the attribution from the link in the search results and did not read the paper. — [Springer, doi:10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z) (secondary / verify)
- Refutation text definition: it states a common misconception, refutes it, and gives the correct explanation (Hynd 2001; Tippett 2010; Mason et al. 2019). Guzzetti et al.'s 1993 meta-analysis concluded that text corrects misconceptions when it is refutational or is combined with cognitive-conflict strategies. — [secondary citation in TUSED article](https://www.tused.org/index.php/tused/article/download/3754/1247/17209) (secondary; Tippett 2010 and Guzzetti 1993 originals not read)
- Tippett's thesis compared refutation and expository passages in 40 Grade 3–4 children (quasi-experimental). — [Tippett, UVic thesis](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/8dd113f0-de32-4114-8d0c-f3e65c3a3cb5)
- **Pickett, Nielson, Marshall, Tanner & Coley 2022** (microbiology-specific). Advanced biology majors in a genetics course; 113 enrolled, 64 analysed; random assignment to reading conditions.
  - Time 1: a refutation / metacognitive reading cut intuitive language in open responses from 81% to 41% (χ² = 9.31, p = 0.002). Plain scientific and teleologically phrased texts gave no significant change.
  - Time 2: two refutation variants. One explained why the misconception is wrong (agreement fell 59% → 21%); the other explained the intuitive reasoning behind it (60% → 7%). Explicit rejection of the misconception rose from 27% to 56%.
  - A single reading did not reliably change how students wrote explanations in their own words. The authors recommend combining refutation text with active learning.
  - Design advice: name the specific misconception, then refute it, using visible headings and bullets.
  - Limitations: small sample, no true no-reading control.
  — [JMBE 2022;23(1):e00220-21, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00220-21](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00220-21)
- Richard et al. 2017 recommend making instructors aware of intuitive reasoning (teleological, essentialist, anthropocentric). They cite calls for cognitive conflict and note that compartmentalised, memorisation-focused teaching is ineffective. — [CBE Life Sci Educ 2017, doi:10.1187/cbe.16-11-0317](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.16-11-0317)
- Peer instruction in medical and dental education (meta-analysis, 17 studies): large within-session conceptual gains (Hedges' g ≥ 1.48) but no performance advantage over other active approaches (p = 0.082). 74% of learners found it helpful. — [Aldalou et al., Med Sci Educ 2026, doi:10.1007/s40670-026-02744-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40670-026-02744-1)
- Interactive cellular-respiration tutorial with constructed-response questions: 841 students at 19 institutions. Scientific ideas increased, but "mixed" thinking persisted, and constructed responses revealed more mixed thinking than multiple choice. — [Uhl et al., CBE Life Sci Educ 2021, doi:10.1187/cbe.20-06-0122](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.20-06-0122)
- POE evidence: no undergraduate microbiology POE study was found. Available evidence is from school biology (Nigeria, n = 280, quasi-experimental), one-group chemistry pre/post designs (Indonesia), and descriptive enzyme work with pre-service teachers. — [IJESSR (Nigeria)](https://jurnal.umsu.ac.id/index.php/ijessr/article/view/17694); [Asia-Pacific Forum Sci Learn Teach 2016](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v17_issue2/gungor/page5.htm)
- Case-based teaching: an interrupted case study on VRSA-1 (horizontal gene transfer) reported "considerable learning gains" in introductory and intermediate microbiology. — [Boury et al., JMBE 2022, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00125-22](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00125-22)
- Briggs et al. 2017 paired each top-five misconception with suggested classroom interventions. — [JMBE 2017, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319)

### Inferences
- An interactive e-book suits a "refutation box + predict-then-reveal" pattern:
  1. Elicit a prediction (a clicker-style multiple-choice question using MCI/HPI-CI-style distractors).
  2. Show data or a simulation (e.g., plating a mixed bacterial population on antibiotic; Luria–Delbrück-style fluctuation logic showing mutants exist before exposure).
  3. Give a refutation text that names the misconception and explains why the intuition is tempting (the stronger Pickett Time 2 variant).
  4. Require a short free-text explanation, since Uhl 2021 and Pickett 2022 show multiple choice hides mixed models.
- Two-tier items (answer + justification, as in the HPI-CI) can be built into end-of-chapter checks and later used to evaluate the book.
- Expect refutation to shift agreement more readily than spontaneous explanation. Give repeated exposure across chapters (e.g., resistance revisited under bacterial genetics, under selective toxicity, and under One Health).

### Gaps
- Tippett 2010 (review in *Int J Sci Math Educ*) and Guzzetti 1993 were not read in original.
- No study found of refutation texts or POE specifically in *medical* students' microbiology, or in Asian medical schools.
- Long-term retention evidence for refutation effects in microbiology is lacking.

## 3. Ecological / microbiome / One Health / planetary health framings: the case for and against, with evidence

### Takeaway
There is strong *conceptual* backing for teaching pathogenicity as context-dependent: Casadevall and Pirofski's damage-response framework and their MISTEACHING host attributes. There is good evidence that a "microbes are malevolent" model forms early and may inhibit learning (Byrne). But I found **no controlled study showing that a microbiome-first or One Health framing improves microbiology understanding or reduces "germ" misconceptions** in medical students. Existing One Health evidence in medical education is mostly cross-sectional self-report surveys.

### Cited Findings
- **Damage-response framework (DRF):** pathogenicity is an outcome of the host–microbe interaction, measured as host damage. The 2015 extension incorporates the microbiota and defines outcomes along a continuum: symbiosis, colonisation, commensalism, latency, disease. — [Casadevall & Pirofski, Infect Immun 2015, doi:10.1128/iai.02627-14](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.02627-14)
- MISTEACHING host-susceptibility attributes. The authors stress "caution in accepting dogma" and say outcomes are predictable only probabilistically. — [Casadevall & Pirofski, Infect Immun 2018, doi:10.1128/iai.00636-17](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.00636-17)
- DRF applied to COVID-19: much of the damage is immune-mediated, which explains differing susceptibility in vulnerable groups. This directly counters "the microbe itself causes all symptoms". — [Pirofski & Casadevall, mBio 2020;11:e01175-20, doi:10.1128/mbio.01175-20](https://doi.org/10.1128/mbio.01175-20)
- Environmental origin of virulence: *Cryptococcus neoformans* virulence factors are traits selected for survival in the environment (e.g., against soil amoebae) that incidentally enable infection. Disease is rare in immunocompetent people despite widespread exposure. — [Casadevall, Virulence 2025, doi:10.1080/21505594.2025.2547090](https://doi.org/10.1080/21505594.2025.2547090); [Vij et al., mSphere 2020, doi:10.1128/msphere.00310-20](https://doi.org/10.1128/msphere.00310-20)
- ASM Council on Microbial Sciences calls for a holistic view of host–microbe biology linking human, animal and environmental health. — [Gestal et al., mSphere 2024, doi:10.1128/msphere.00256-24](https://doi.org/10.1128/msphere.00256-24)
- Case *for* an early counter to the "malevolent microbe" view: Byrne found it dominant from age 7 to 14 and argued it may inhibit later learning. — [Byrne 2011, doi:10.1080/09500693.2010.536999](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2010.536999); [Byrne thesis](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/466291)
- Personal microbiome analysis in an undergraduate course (Brigham Young University) significantly increased engagement and interest in microbiome units. It measured attitudes to the course, not to bacteria. — [PLoS ONE 2018 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/7c1865c9a7d441f6a22aa3825b240015)
- Survey of University of Jordan students on microbiota knowledge: a baseline survey, not an intervention. — [PMC8708365](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8708365/)
- **One Health in medical education.** Mostly cross-sectional survey evidence:
  - At a Turkish medical faculty, about 30% of students had heard of One Health, and only 16.5% cited medical education as the source.
  - Zoonosis knowledge was higher in sixth-year than first-year students (97.1% vs 88.3%), but AMR knowledge did not differ (89.3% vs 92.1%).
  — [Türkiye Klinikleri J Med Ethics](https://turkiyeklinikleri.com/article/en-bursa-uludag-universitesi-veteriner-ve-tip-fakulteleri-ogrencilerinin-zoonoz-hastaliklar-hakkindaki-farkindalik-duzeylerinin-degerlendirilmesi-85233.html); [BMC Med Educ 2025 table](https://bmcmededuc.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12909-025-08066-x/tables/4) (secondary / verify; the search summary may have merged two studies)
- MD/DVM dual-degree students had the most accurate understanding of One Health competencies (AMR, zoonoses) compared with MD-only students. — [PMC6000817](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6000817) (secondary / verify)
- African 26-country survey of higher-education students: key gaps in One Health knowledge, e.g., awareness of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (65.3%). — [PMC12087165](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12087165/)
- The WHO 2015 survey found 73% of the public already agree farmers should use fewer antibiotics in food animals (China 83%). One Health / AMR links are intuitive to the public. — [WHO 2015](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance)

### Inferences
- **Case for an ecological-first opening:**
  - It directly targets the documented malevolent/anthropocentric model (Byrne; Richard 2017).
  - It makes "infection ≠ disease" and opportunism the default logic, not exceptions. The DRF and MISTEACHING give a reusable explanatory frame for every organism group, so diversity becomes "same questions, different organisms" instead of a catalogue.
  - It previews pathology (host damage) and immunology (host response) that come next in the NUS sequence.
  - Local data (asymptomatic enterovirus in childcare; asymptomatic Nipah seropositivity) make it concrete.
- **Case against / risks:**
  - No outcome evidence that it improves learning.
  - Microbiome content can drift into speculative "dysbiosis causes everything" claims.
  - Novices may over-correct to "microbes are mostly good, so infection is unusual".
  - Time pressure in a 3-month block.
  - Medical students may see it as less exam-relevant.
  - A balanced design would use ecology as the organising frame (where do organisms live, what do they need, what happens when the context changes) and anchor each concept to a clinical consequence.
- One Health is best placed as a recurring lens (zoonosis case boxes; AMR across human, animal and environment) rather than a standalone chapter. Its evidence base in medical education is thin, but it is policy-endorsed and locally vivid (Nipah, Wolbachia, melioidosis in soil).
- The e-book could be evaluated with pre/post HPI-CI or MCI items plus a short "microbe attitude" item set, which would fill the evidence gap.

### Gaps
- No controlled or pre/post study found comparing microbiome-first or ecology-first sequencing with traditional taxonomic sequencing for medical students.
- No evidence found on whether ecological framing reduces "germ" misconceptions in adults or university students.
- Planetary health / climate and emerging-infection framing in medical microbiology teaching: no specific studies retrieved.

## 4. Teaching the oxygen spectrum and other physiological groupings conceptually

### Takeaway
"Bacteria need oxygen" is a documented novice misconception: 19.5% of pre-course explanations in the MCI study, alongside "fermentation requires oxygen". Metabolism has the weakest learning gains on the MCI. The textbook explanation for obligate anaerobes (they lack superoxide dismutase and catalase) is a 1970 correlation that has been overinterpreted. Current understanding (Lu & Imlay 2021) is that anaerobes have most ROS defences but depend on low-potential metal and radical enzymes that O₂ and ROS poison directly. This is a richer, mechanistic bridge from cell-biology knowledge of respiration and ROS. I found no microbiology-education paper specifically on teaching the oxygen spectrum.

### Cited Findings
- 19.5% of coded pre-course explanations held that oxygen is required for, or speeds up, bacterial growth. "Fermentation requires oxygen" was a related error. — [Briggs et al., JMBE 2017, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319)
- Students also believe prokaryotes have mitochondria for respiration. — [Briggs et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319)
- MCI: metabolism questions had the weakest learning gains (overall normalised gain 0.15). — [Paustian et al., JMBE 2017, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1320](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1320)
- Cellular respiration: undergraduates hold mixed scientific and non-scientific ideas that persist after a tutorial. — [Uhl et al. 2021, doi:10.1187/cbe.20-06-0122](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.20-06-0122)
- Lu & Imlay review:
  - The popular hypothesis that anaerobes failed to evolve ROS defences is challenged; anaerobes have most of the same defences as aerobes, and many tolerate substantial O₂.
  - Anaerobic metabolism relies on radical chemistry and low-potential metal centres that are "inherently susceptible" to direct poisoning by O₂ and ROS.
  - Anaerobes cope by limiting O₂ disruption or by rapidly restoring function afterwards.
  — [Nat Rev Microbiol 2021;19:774–785, doi:10.1038/s41579-021-00583-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41579-021-00583-y)
- *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* (a dominant gut anaerobe): its oxygen sensitivity lies not in its defensive systems but in the rate of endogenous oxidant formation. Aeration inactivated iron–sulfur and iron enzymes, and overproducing SOD protected them, so superoxide is a major element of its O₂ sensitivity. — [PMC5889672](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889672)
- History: McCord & Fridovich (1970) noted many obligate anaerobes lack SOD and catalase, and the correlation entered textbooks. *E. coli* lacking SOD loses biosynthetic and catabolic functions under air. Obligate anaerobes tolerate roughly <0.5 to 8% O₂. — [Wikipedia: Obligate anaerobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obligate_anaerobe) (tertiary; use only for orientation)

### Inferences
- **Conceptual sequence building on prior cell biology:**
  1. Recall the electron transport chain, with O₂ as terminal electron acceptor, and mitochondrial ROS as a by-product (students know this).
  2. Ask what a cell does if there is no O₂: fermentation, or anaerobic respiration with other acceptors.
  3. Ask why O₂ would harm a cell. ROS form when O₂ meets reduced enzymes, and the low-potential Fe–S enzymes that anaerobes rely on are especially vulnerable.
  4. Place organisms on a spectrum. The categories are positions on a continuous tolerance/use axis, not separate boxes:
     - Obligate aerobes: need O₂ and cope with ROS.
     - Facultative anaerobes: switch metabolism; e.g., *E. coli*, *S. aureus*, most Enterobacterales.
     - Microaerophiles: need some O₂ but are damaged by atmospheric levels; e.g., *Campylobacter*, *Helicobacter*.
     - Aerotolerant anaerobes: ferment regardless of O₂; e.g., streptococci.
     - Obligate anaerobes: e.g., *Bacteroides*, *Clostridium*.
- **POE hook:** predict the growth pattern of different organisms in a thioglycollate tube (top, throughout, bands, bottom), then reveal it. This classic visual suits an interactive e-book and directly confronts the 19.5% "all bacteria need oxygen" idea.
- **Pathology link (comes next in the curriculum):** necrotic or ischaemic tissue, abscess cores and deep wounds have low O₂ and low redox potential. Anaerobes thrive there, and their growth adds further damage. This frames gas gangrene (*C. perfringens*), tetanus in deep puncture wounds, aspiration-pneumonia abscesses and diabetic-foot infections as "ecology of a niche", not facts to memorise.
- **Microbiota link:** the colon is mostly obligate anaerobes, the skin mostly aerobes and facultatives. Oxygen gradients explain where commensals live, and so which ones cause endogenous infection when barriers fail.
- The same "what does it need / where can it live" approach extends to other groupings: temperature (why *Cryptococcus* or dimorphic fungi switch form at 37 °C), obligate intracellular lifestyle (chlamydiae, rickettsiae, all viruses), and the cell envelope (Gram stain as a window on the wall, previewing selective toxicity).
- Caution for authors: avoid stating "anaerobes die because they lack SOD and catalase" as the full mechanism. Present it as the historical hypothesis and update it, which is itself a good "science changes" moment.

### Gaps
- No education paper found on teaching the oxygen spectrum or anaerobe pathophysiology specifically.
- Lu & Imlay full text not read. The 1970 McCord–Fridovich paper is cited only via a secondary source.

## 5. Bridging from cell biology to microbiology: prokaryote vs eukaryote, central dogma, "selective toxicity"

### Takeaway
Entering microbiology students carry errors from cell and molecular biology into microbiology: prokaryotes have mitochondria, horizontal gene transfer is ignored, RNA polymerase and primase are confused, and all prokaryotes respond alike to penicillin. These make a direct cell-biology → microbiology bridge necessary. I found no education study of "selective toxicity" as an organising concept. Its value here is an inference from the misconception data and from the curriculum sequence.

### Cited Findings
- Pre-course misconceptions with direct cell-biology roots:
  - Prokaryotes have organelles (mitochondria, chloroplasts).
  - Similar gene sequences arise only vertically (19.4%), ignoring horizontal gene transfer.
  - RNA, RNA polymerase and primase roles are confused (19%).
  - All prokaryotes respond to penicillin the same way ("they are similar organisms").
  - Mutations are always harmful.
  - A human gene would be expressed in *E. coli* because expression is assumed to be the same in all cells.
  — [Briggs et al., JMBE 2017, doi:10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319)
- Introductory biology students hold five or six distinct ideas per response about genetic information flow, mixing normative and non-normative ideas. — [Uhl et al., Biochem Mol Biol Educ 2021, doi:10.1002/bmb.21483](https://doi.org/10.1002/bmb.21483)
- An interrupted case study on VRSA-1 introduces horizontal gene transfer to early students, with reported learning gains. — [Boury et al., JMBE 2022, doi:10.1128/jmbe.00125-22](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00125-22)
- Students and laypeople confuse viruses with bacteria and treat viruses as cells or as living. Teaching the basic nature of viruses fixes the classification but not downstream inferences (e.g., about treatment). — [Univ. Arizona dissertation](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/290010); [J. Pendas 2024](https://journal.unpas.ac.id/index.php/pendas/article/view/63305)
- 39.5% of medical students in a 20-study review believed antibiotics work against viral infections. — [Rev. Salud Pública review](https://revistas.urosario.edu.co/index.php/revsalud/article/view/13449) (secondary / verify)

### Inferences
- **"Selective toxicity" as a unifying question:** "What does this organism have, or do, that my cells don't?" asked of each organism group, without teaching drug names.
  - Bacteria: peptidoglycan wall, 70S ribosomes, bacterial DNA gyrase/topoisomerases, folate synthesis. Many targets, so many antibacterials.
  - Fungi: eukaryotes with ergosterol instead of cholesterol and a chitin/glucan wall. Fewer targets, so fewer antifungals and more host toxicity. This also explains why fungi are not plants (no cellulose wall or chloroplasts; closer to animals).
  - Protozoa and helminths: eukaryotes, sometimes multicellular. Even fewer unique targets.
  - Viruses: no metabolism or ribosomes of their own; they hijack host machinery. Targets are restricted to virus-specific enzymes and steps (polymerases, proteases, entry, uncoating). This directly explains why antibiotics do nothing to viruses: there is no wall or ribosome to hit.
- This turns "antibiotics don't kill viruses" from a rule into a consequence of cell biology students already know. That is the kind of explanation-based refutation that Pickett 2022 found most effective.
- **Central-dogma bridge for resistance:** mutation (replication error), horizontal gene transfer (plasmids, transduction, transformation) and selection pressure are all prior-knowledge concepts applied at population scale. They should be taught explicitly as population-level change, to counter anthropocentric and teleological reasoning.
- **Scale bridge:** use benchmarks students already hold (red blood cell about 7–8 µm; typical bacterium about 1–2 µm; typical virus about 20–300 nm; ribosome about 20–30 nm) with an interactive zoom. This targets the "all invisible things are the same size" misconception (Tretter et al. 2006, via secondary source). The size values are standard textbook figures, not from a retrieved source.

### Gaps
- No education research found evaluating "selective toxicity" as a threshold or organising concept, or comparing it with drug-by-drug teaching.
- No study found on fungi-as-plants misconception prevalence.

## 6. Singapore / regional anchoring examples

### Takeaway
Singapore has well-documented, peer-reviewed local cases that map onto foundation concepts:
- Wolbachia releases: ecology and symbiosis used for vector control.
- Nipah 1999: zoonosis and One Health.
- SARS 2003: transmission, superspreading and infection control.
- HFMD: enteroviruses, asymptomatic carriage and childcare transmission.
- Melioidosis: environmental soil saprophyte, opportunism and host factors (diabetes).
- Local AMR beliefs: 62.5% think the body becomes resistant.

### Cited Findings
**Dengue and Wolbachia**
- Singapore uses *Wolbachia*-mediated sterility: it releases male *Ae. aegypti* carrying wAlbB-SG, which suppresses the mosquito population. This differs from the "replacement" strategy used elsewhere.
- Lim JT et al. synthetic control study, 2018–2022, in Yishun and Tampines (expanding releases) and Bukit Batok and Choa Chu Kang (targeted). About 607,872 people lived in intervention areas versus about 3.89 million in controls. The Gravitrap index fell as *Wolbachia* coverage rose.
— [Lim JT et al., Lancet Microbe 2024;5:e422–e432, doi:10.1016/S2666-5247(23)00397-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2666-5247(23)00397-X)
- An arXiv version reports a 57% decline in total dengue incidence and a 64% decline in clustered dengue incidence. I could not confirm the published headline efficacy figure. — [arXiv 2311.09754](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09754v1)
- An emulated cluster-randomised (test-negative) analysis was also published. — [Lim et al., J Travel Med 2024, PMC11500660](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11500660/)
- NEA (July 2021) reported up to 98% suppression of urban *Ae. aegypti* and up to 88% fewer dengue cases at the Yishun, Tampines, Choa Chu Kang and Bukit Batok study sites. These are "up to" maxima against non-randomised comparison areas, and earlier figures were 58–74% and 65–80%. — [NEA press release](https://www.nea.gov.sg/media/news/news/index/project-wolbachia-singapore-suppresses-aedes-aegypti-mosquito-population-and-reduces-dengue-cases-at-release-sites)

**Nipah 1999**
- Singapore abattoir workers were infected by pigs imported from Malaysia, giving about 11 cases and 1 death. Serology found 22 infected, of whom about 12 (54%) were symptomatic, all with direct pig contact and no human-to-human spread. — [Epidemiol Infect survey of risk groups](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/145043E616F1ED495CEA6D49A2E07AA9/S0950268801006422a.pdf/a-survey-of-nipah-virus-infection-among-various-risk-groups-in-singapore.pdf)
- No new cases after abattoirs were closed on 19 March 1999. — [CDC MMWR 1999](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00057012.htm)
- Malaysia: 265 cases and 105 deaths; more than a million pigs culled. — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998%E2%80%931999_Malaysia_Nipah_virus_outbreak) (tertiary)

**SARS 2003**
- Singapore had 238 cases and 33 deaths (13.9%), February–May 2003. Five superspreaders were identified; the index traveller admitted to Tan Tock Seng Hospital infected 24 people. Infections clustered in healthcare workers and their families. — [Chan KP, Environ Health Prev Med 2005](https://environhealthprevmed.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1007/BF02897699.pdf)
- Daily temperature monitoring of healthcare workers identified cases early, but temperature screening of children and airport travellers did not. — [Tan C, Ann Acad Med Singap 2006;35:345–9 (ASPR TRACIE abstract)](https://asprtracie.hhs.gov/technical-resources/resource/1621/sars-in-singapore-key-lessons-from-an-epidemic)

**Hand, foot and mouth disease (HFMD)**
- The 2000 EV-A71 epidemic caused 3,790 reported cases from September to October, with 3 HFMD deaths. — [Emerg Infect Dis, PMC2873753](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2873753/)
- Incidence rose from 125.5 to 435.9 per 100,000 between 2001 and 2007, and institutional outbreaks rose from 167 to 1,723. — [Ann Acad Med Singap 2009;38:106–12](https://annals.edu.sg/epidemiology-and-control-of-hand-foot-and-mouth-disease-in-singapore-2001-2007/)
- 2013–2018: CV-A6 caused 41% of 101 genotyped cases versus about 7% for EV-A71, and 17.1% of childcare children carried enterovirus without symptoms. — [PMC7901731](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7901731/)

**Melioidosis (*Burkholderia pseudomallei*)**
- A soil and freshwater Gram-negative bacterium. Singapore incidence fell over 2003–2014, unlike in northern Australia and Thailand, and the decline was not explained by climate factors. Diabetes was present in 56.7% of patients versus 19.3% of an age-matched general population; renal impairment in 15.3% versus 2.0%. — [Pang et al., Emerg Infect Dis 2018;24(1)](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/24/1/16-1449)
- Singapore General Hospital, 16-year series of 219 cases: 63% diabetic, 83% male, mortality over 15%, with more than a third of deaths before diagnosis. — [BMC Infect Dis 2018, PMC6158812](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6158812/)
- 1989–1996: mean incidence 1.7 per 100,000, case fatality 39.5%, no correlation with rainfall. — [Ann Acad Med Singap 1998;27:478–84](https://annals.edu.sg/epidemiological-surveillance-of-melioidosis-in-singapore/)

**Local AMR beliefs**
- 62.5% of Singapore residents think the body becomes resistant. — [Lim JM et al., BMJ Open 2021](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2020-048157)

### Inferences
Each example below sets the organism group or concept against the misconception it targets.
- **Wolbachia:** a bacterium as a tool and an intracellular symbiont, against "all microbes are harmful". It also shows host-dependent outcomes (cytoplasmic incompatibility), vectors and arboviruses, and population ecology. Students may already know the green release boxes in HDB estates.
- **Melioidosis:** an environmental saprophyte, against "pathogens are adapted to infect humans" (compare *Cryptococcus*, Casadevall). It shows host factors (diabetes), the soil–human interface, and Gram-negative intrinsic resistance (previews selective toxicity).
- **Nipah:** bat → pig → human spillover, against "infection = disease" (asymptomatic seropositives). It shows One Health and a policy response (abattoir closure, import bans).
- **SARS 2003 / COVID-19:** airborne and contact transmission, superspreading, healthcare-associated transmission, and immune-mediated damage (DRF, COVID-19 mBio paper). It also shows the limits of fever screening, against "the microbe causes the fever directly".
- **HFMD:** non-enveloped enteroviruses, faecal–oral and respiratory spread, asymptomatic carriage, serotype shifts (EV-A71 to CV-A6), and childcare settings.
- **Local AMR survey:** a refutation-text opener ("62.5% of Singaporeans believe… here's why that's not how it works").

### Gaps
- Hawker-centre food safety: no peer-reviewed Singapore source retrieved. NEA or Singapore Food Agency outbreak reports would be the place to look (e.g., salmonellosis or *Bacillus cereus* clusters).
- COVID-19 Singapore-specific epidemiology was not retrieved in this pass. The dormitory outbreaks of 2020 are a likely anchor.
- The published headline efficacy figure from Lim JT et al. 2024 (Lancet Microbe) was not confirmed; only the arXiv figures (57% / 64%) were.
- No sources retrieved on parasites in Singapore (e.g., imported malaria; *Angiostrongylus*), which would counter the "parasites only in the tropics / not here" idea.
