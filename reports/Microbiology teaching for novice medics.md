# Teach microbial logic, not a bug parade

The evidence says to make the *dimension of diversity*, not the organism, the unit of learning. Students should first learn a small set of mechanistic attributes: cell envelope, energy and oxygen use, genome and replication strategy, intracellular versus extracellular lifestyle, transmission, and how host damage arises. They should then meet diversity through a handful of contrasting exemplars, with retrieval, prediction and refutation doing most of the teaching. No controlled trial has compared concept-first with taxonomy-first general microbiology for medical novices, so this recommendation rests on two things. The first is expert consensus: the 2026 AMSMIC objectives deliberately make the 64 foundational objectives pathogen-free, and the 2024 ASM guidelines reduce the field to 29 fundamental statements. The second is learning-science findings carried over from other domains. Those findings are strong: comparing cases beats studying them one at a time (d ≈ 0.50); interleaved practice helps category discrimination (g ≈ 0.42, or 0.67 for visual material); retrieval practice gives g ≈ 0.5–0.6; refutation texts give g ≈ 0.41; and teaching causal mechanisms protects diagnostic knowledge after a delay (62% vs 51% at one week). Concept inventories show that novices fail most on what a bug parade skips. Metabolism shows almost no learning gain (normalised gain 0.15). Students do not grasp that viruses depend on host cells (27% correct after instruction), and most believe resistance arises because the body, or a bacterium's "need", drives it. In Singapore, 62.5% of residents believe the body becomes resistant. Engagement formats such as games, case-based learning and virtual labs mostly show better satisfaction, or equivalence to conventional teaching, rather than better learning. In the e-book, every interactive format should therefore carry a retrieval, comparison or prediction mechanism, and every case should be re-scoped from "diagnose and treat" to "explain and investigate". Many specific choices are design inference rather than tested findings: the profile template, the number of exemplars, the chapter order, and the LLM chats. The report labels them as such.

## Experts have retired the bug parade without trial evidence

The strongest support for a concept-organised foundation block is a consensus document, not an outcome study. The **2026 AMSMIC medical microbiology objectives** (Bauler, Arvidson et al., *JMBE* 27(2), doi:10.1128/jmbe.00042-26) were built with a hybrid Nominal Group Technique/Delphi process. The process started from **679 content areas** in the AMSMIC 2009 and IDSA 2010 documents. It split the curriculum into **64 foundational objectives that name no specific pathogen** and **125 systems objectives**. The systems objectives are grouped into six organ systems plus blood/systemic and zoonotic/opportunistic infections, and framed around clinical presentation. The foundational set is organised under bacteriology (structure, metabolism, genetics), virology (structure and replication, genetics, pathogenesis), mycology and parasitology, disease processes (pathogenesis and host–pathogen interaction), and general diagnosis, treatment and prevention ([PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)). The authors say standalone courses often fall back on a traditional bug-parade approach that weakens clinical recall and interest, and that medical microbiology "must move beyond the 'bug parade'" ([PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)). They also say plainly that the objectives are consensus, that most sit at lower Bloom's levels, and that summary objectives are meant to prompt case-based, higher-order application.

AMSMIC's foundational/systems split matches the NUS sequence almost exactly: a pathogen-free foundation now, presentation-organised organ blocks later. One detail is easy to miss. **AMSMIC still organises its foundational layer by organism group.** The move away from the bug parade is pathogen-free objectives inside each group, not abandoning bacteria/viruses/fungi/parasites as headings. The foundational set also includes a pathogen-free "diagnosis, treatment and prevention" heading. The NUS brief defers systems-level diagnosis and treatment, but general principles fall inside the foundational scope: how microbes are seen and grown, why selective toxicity is possible, how sterilisation and vaccination work in principle. That boundary is an inference. The research notes captured only the AMSMIC headings, not the text of the 64 objectives, and did not retrieve the Appendix 3 pathogen list. Both should be checked directly against the paper before chapters are finalised.

The **2024 ASM undergraduate curriculum guidelines** (Boury et al., *JMBE* 25(3), doi:10.1128/jmbe.00126-24) supply the conceptual spine AMSMIC lacks. They set out **29 fundamental statements across six areas**: evolution, cell structure and function, metabolic pathways, information flow, microbial ecology, and impact of microbes. They also extend the language to eukaryotic microbes and acellular agents ([Boury et al. 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11636387/fullTextXML)). Several statements together give a ready-made narrative for a medical foundation block. Microbial structures are targets for both antimicrobials and host immunity (FS6). Obligate intracellular microbes require living host cells (FS11). Most microbes are beneficial or neutral (FS22). Only a minority are pathogens (FS28). Microbial damage is limited by host and external factors, including the microbiome and immunity (FS29). ASM presents these as recommendations, not a mandate ([ASM](https://asm.org/guideline/asm-curriculum-guidelines-for-undergraduate-microb)).

Published medical-school redesigns show how far practice lags behind the rhetoric. A survey of US preclinical microbiology (104 of 142 schools responding) found that course directors struggle with integration and shrinking hours ([Melber et al. 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC11000598%20OR%20PMCID:PMC11898690%20OR%20DOI:10.1093/cid/ciw262%20OR%20DOI:10.1128/jmbe.v10.i1.97&resultType=core&format=json)). AMSMIC reports that **45% of schools had fully integrated microbiology** by then, against preclerkship phases shrinking to 12–18 months ([PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)). Dell Medical School's flipped, team-taught microbiology–immunology–pharmacology–ID unit is a well-described innovative course, yet it still ran week by week through Gram-positives, Gram-negatives, viruses, then helminths and protozoa. Exemplars were chosen for clinical prevalence or seriousness, and no comparative outcome data were reported ([Steinel et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8368113/)). The IDSA's 2010 preclinical guidance attacked PowerPoint-and-MCQ teaching and called for a national consensus on factual content to reduce overload ([IDSA commentary](https://einstein.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/commentary-idsa-guidelines-for-improving-the-teaching-of-preclini-3/)). The fair reading is that the field innovates in pedagogy and exemplar curation while keeping the organism-class skeleton. Published outcomes are satisfaction and feasibility. A claim that concept-first teaching *improves learning* in medical microbiology is still expert opinion, supported only indirectly by the learning science below.

## Novices stumble on metabolism, viruses and selection, the topics catalogues skip

Two validated concept inventories agree on where introductory students fail. Both were validated on US undergraduates, not medical students. On the **Microbiology Concept Inventory** (23 items, 10 institutions), students gained everywhere except metabolism, where the normalised gain was only **0.15**. The weakest post-test items were metabolic diversity (**0.24** correct, no gain), genome manipulation and vaccine function (0.25), **viral replication's dependence on host cells (0.27)**, information-flow differences across domains (0.29), and prokaryote–eukaryote cell properties (0.36). The inventory mean was about 0.55 ([Paustian et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976036)). The **Host–Pathogen Interactions Concept Inventory** shows the same pattern. Its metabolism item scored 9–27% correct before instruction and 9–26% after. Its Gram-stain item scored as low as 3% before instruction in one general microbiology class. On antibiotic resistance, 38 of 68 students said selection acts on genotype alone and only one said phenotype ([Marbach-Ad et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577151/)). Distractor choices on that resistance item correlated closely across institutions, which suggests a shared misconception rather than local teaching failures ([Stevens et al. 2017](https://doaj.org/article/6a3203f4342c4b76af586e7503688783)).

The development data for the MCI show what students bring in. Among 743 students starting introductory microbiology, the commonest coded misconceptions were that bacteria must be exposed to an antibiotic before they can mutate to resistance (**43%**), that vaccines must cause disease to work (**21.7%**), that oxygen is required for or speeds up bacterial growth (**19.5%**), that similar genes arise only by vertical descent (19.4%), and confusion between RNA, RNA polymerase and primase (19%). Students also credited prokaryotes with mitochondria and assumed all prokaryotes respond alike to penicillin ([Briggs et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.v18i3.1319)). Most of these errors carry over from cell biology, the one subject NUS students have just completed. That makes an explicit cell-biology-to-microbiology bridge a priority rather than a courtesy.

Resistance misconceptions are the best quantified and the most locally vivid. In a careful US study, **81% of students** agreed that bacteria develop mutations in order to become resistant and survive (teleological reasoning). **57%** attributed resistance to changes within humans (anthropocentric). Even advanced majors held the teleological view at 74%. And while 91% called resistance an example of evolution, only 10–28% mentioned natural selection ([Richard, Coley & Tanner 2017](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.16-11-0317)). WHO's 12-country survey found that **76%** of respondents thought the body becomes resistant, with Viet Nam highest at 86% ([WHO 2015](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance)). In Singapore, **62.5% of 706 residents** gave the same answer ([Lim et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2020-048157)). Medical students are not immune: a pooled review of 13,997 medical students reported that 39.5% thought antibiotics work against viral infections, a figure taken from a secondary summary that should be verified ([review](https://revistas.urosario.edu.co/index.php/revsalud/article/view/13449)). Upstream of all this sits a "malevolent microbe" model. Children aged 7 to 14 consistently saw microorganisms as essentially harmful, a view that shaped how they classified living things and that may inhibit later learning ([Byrne 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/09500693.2010.536999)). Viruses are routinely treated as living cells or small bacteria. Teaching the nature of viruses fixes how students classify them but not what they infer from it ([Univ. Arizona dissertation](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/290010)).

Two cautions apply. The threshold-concept framework, the obvious tool for naming "troublesome" ideas, has barely been applied to medical microbiology. No empirical threshold-concept study of medical students exists ([Neve et al. 2016](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/8594)). Any list of microbiology threshold concepts is therefore an inference from inventory data. The likely candidates are selection on phenotype, metabolic and oxygen diversity, information flow across domains, viral dependence on the host, and pathogenicity as an outcome rather than a trait. The other caution concerns validity. Five years of MCI data at one US university showed score differences by ethnicity that the authors read as possible bias ([Rennpferd et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00110-22)). No Singapore medical-student misconception data were found. A short pre-course diagnostic built from MCI- and HPI-CI-style two-tier items would both calibrate the book and fill a publishable gap.

One textbook explanation itself needs correcting. The standard line that obligate anaerobes die because they lack superoxide dismutase and catalase comes from a 1970 correlation that has been over-read. Current work shows that anaerobes have most of the same reactive-oxygen defences as aerobes. Their vulnerability lies in low-potential metal centres and radical enzymes that O₂ and ROS poison directly ([Lu & Imlay 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41579-021-00583-y); [PMC5889672](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889672)). This gives a richer mechanistic bridge from what students already know about the electron transport chain and mitochondrial ROS, and it offers a ready-made "science revises itself" moment.

## Causal mechanisms outlast feature lists after a one-week delay

### Pre-train the attribute vocabulary before any organism appears

Cognitive load theory explains why the catalogue fails novices. Microbiology is high in **element interactivity**. Each organism bundles many attributes that interact causally: a Gram-negative outer membrane carries LPS, which acts as endotoxin and drives the host inflammation that general pathology describes. Whether that bundle overwhelms working memory depends on whether the learner already holds schemas that chunk it ([van Merriënboer & Sweller 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03498.x); [Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1)). Two of Mayer's multimedia principles address this directly. **Pre-training**, teaching the component concepts before the full system, was supported in 13 of 16 tests with a median d = 0.75. **Segmenting**, learner-paced chunks, was supported in 10 of 10 with a median d = 0.79 ([Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258)). Both effects come from short laboratory lessons on topics such as lightning and brakes, not medical courses. The expertise reversal effect adds a time dimension. Worked examples and integrated guidance help novices but lose their value, or backfire, as knowledge grows ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)). Year-1 students start the block as true novices and finish it as something more, so the scaffolding should fade within the book itself.

The applied inference is straightforward. Teach each attribute dimension once, with its mechanism and its consequence, before organisms appear: envelope type, oxygen relationship, spores and capsules, genome type and envelope for viruses, yeast versus mould, protozoan versus helminth. An organism then becomes a combination of known chunks rather than a new list of isolated facts. No study has tested pre-training in a microbiology course. The rationale is CLT applied to the content's structure.

### Mechanism-first knowledge is what later becomes clinical expertise

Medical-education research on expertise describes a developmental sequence. Students first build elaborated causal networks of biomedical knowledge. Clinical experience then "encapsulates" that knowledge into higher-level concepts, which mature into illness scripts and finally instance scripts ([Schmidt & Rikers 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02915.x); [Rikers et al. 2000](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/knowledge-encapsulation-and-the-intermediate-effect/)). Illness scripts have three standard slots: enabling conditions, fault and consequences ([Custers 2015](https://doi.org/10.3109/0142159X.2014.956052)). Pre-clinical novices sit at the first stage. The job of a foundation block is to build the causal substrate that later blocks will encapsulate, not to hand over finished scripts.

The most directly relevant experiments come from Woods, Norman and colleagues. Learners taught basic-science mechanisms and learners taught epidemiological feature lists both scored **70%** immediately after learning. **One week later, the mechanism group held at 62% while the feature-list group fell to 51%** ([Woods et al. 2006](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1608618)). A follow-up found better delayed performance after learning a causal model (89% vs 78%), and greater reliance on causal information after the delay ([Woods, Brooks & Norman 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10459-006-9055-x)). Novices taught integrated causal mechanisms also judged the relative importance of clinical features better ([Lisk, Agur & Woods 2016](https://reference-global.com/article/10.1007/S40037-016-0268-2)). The broader "cognitive integration" argument holds that basic science helps most when it is taught as the causal explanation of features, not as a parallel set of facts ([Kulasegaram et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0b013e3182a45def)). These studies used neurological and rheumatological disorders, not infections, so their application to microbiology is assumed rather than shown. Still, they are the closest experimental evidence that a "because" attached to each microbial attribute is worth the extra words.

### The damage-response framework bridges general pathology and immunology

Because general pathology precedes microbiology at NUS and immunology runs alongside it, the organising frame should make host damage the shared currency. The **Pirofski–Casadevall damage-response framework** was devised in 1999 as a teaching tool for a pathogenesis course. It holds that disease is defined by the amount of host damage, which can come from microbial factors, the host response, or both ([Nosanchuk 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11898690/)). The 2015 extension adds the microbiota and places outcomes on a continuum of symbiosis, colonisation, commensalism, latency and disease ([Casadevall & Pirofski 2015](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.02627-14)). The 2018 **MISTEACHING** set names eleven host-side reasons why the same exposure produces different outcomes: Microbiome, Inoculum, Sex, Temperature, Environment, Age, Chance, History, Immunity, Nutrition, Genetics ([Casadevall & Pirofski 2018](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.00636-17)). Applied to COVID-19, the framework shows that much of the damage is immune-mediated ([Pirofski & Casadevall 2020](https://doi.org/10.1128/mbio.01175-20)). That directly counters the intuition that the microbe itself causes every symptom. The HPI-CI likewise treats microbiology and immunology as one conceptual domain, and AMSMIC explicitly recommends collaborative teaching where its objectives overlap with pathology and immunology ([Marbach-Ad et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577151/); [PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)).

An ecological framing also has a principled case behind it. It targets the documented malevolent-microbe and anthropocentric models, and it makes "infection is not disease" the default rather than the exception. Singapore supplies concrete anchors. **17.1% of childcare children carried enterovirus without HFMD symptoms** ([PMC7901731](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7901731/)). In the 1999 Nipah outbreak, only about 12 of 22 seropositive abattoir workers were symptomatic ([Epidemiol Infect](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/145043E616F1ED495CEA6D49A2E07AA9/S0950268801006422a.pdf/a-survey-of-nipah-virus-infection-among-various-risk-groups-in-singapore.pdf)). Melioidosis, caused by a soil saprophyte, shows host factors at work: 56.7% of Singapore patients had diabetes, against 19.3% of an age-matched general population ([Pang et al. 2018](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/24/1/16-1449)). The honest counterweight is that **no controlled study shows that a microbiome-first or One Health framing improves understanding or reduces "germ" misconceptions** in medical students. One Health evidence in medical education is mostly cross-sectional self-report. Microbiome content also drifts easily into speculative "dysbiosis explains everything" claims, and novices may over-correct to "infection is unusual". The balanced position is to use ecology as the *question frame* (where does it live, what does it need, what changes when context changes?) and to tie each answer to a consequence for host damage.

## Six contrast sets can carry the diversity a catalogue cannot

The learning science of comparison explains how to convey diversity without covering it. A meta-analysis of **57 experiments and 336 tests** found that actively comparing cases produced better learning than studying the same cases singly or in sequence, **d = 0.50**. The benefit was largest for perceptual content and when the principle was stated *after* the comparison rather than before ([Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn 2013](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/ContrastingCasesMeta-AlfieriEtAl2013.pdf)). A secondary summary reports 0.60, so the exact figure should be checked. Learners who compared two cases were nearly three times more likely to transfer the underlying principle ([Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner 1999](https://kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/1999/analogical-encoding-facilitates-knowledge-transfer-in-negotiation?p=1)). Analysing the same cases separately was no better than no training ([Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner 2003](https://business.illinois.edu/loewenstein/papers/Loewensteinetal%20AMLE03.pdf)). Analysing contrasting cases before a lecture prepared students to learn more from it ([Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4)). The implication is pointed: a static comparison table is studying, not comparing. The benefit comes from comparison *activities* in which learners must find the discriminating feature themselves.

Interleaving adds a second, related mechanism. Across 59 studies, mixing exemplars of different categories gave **g = 0.42** overall and **g = 0.67 for paintings and other visual material**. The effect was stronger when categories resembled each other. For word lists blocking won (g = −0.39), and for expository text the effect was unreliable ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). Showing exemplars side by side matched the benefit of interleaving, which points to discriminative contrast as the active ingredient ([Kang & Pashler 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1801)). Learners consistently believe blocking works better even when it does not ([Kornell & Bjork 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x)). In medical education, 40 novice medical students learned chest-radiograph patterns better when the patterns were interleaved ([RSNA 2014](https://archive.rsna.org/2014/14002540.html)). ECG work suggests interleaving pays off only after some initial mastery ([Perspect Med Educ](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40037-017-0365-x)). No interleaving study has used microbiology content. The defensible rule, an inference from these boundary conditions, is *block to learn, interleave to practise*. The explanatory chapter on the Gram-negative envelope stays coherent. The image-sorting and discrimination practice that follows mixes categories.

How exemplars are chosen is a matter of published practice, not evidence. AMSMIC favours common pathogens plus rarer severe ones, and asks schools to add regional and emerging organisms ([PMC13520675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520675/)). Steinel chose exemplars for prevalence or seriousness. Indiana's team-based learning limited itself to eight prevalent pathogens ([IMPRS](https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/IMPRS/article/view/24517)). Graduate pathogenesis courses at Kentucky and elsewhere instead pick organisms that best illustrate a lifestyle or strategy ([UKY](https://www.as.uky.edu/node/1047377)). **No study has tested the optimal number of exemplars** or a "prototype, then contrast" design in medical microbiology. The inference that follows is a small diversity matrix. Each axis is taught through a near-neighbour contrast, and exemplars are chosen to be both clinically common or severe and clean illustrations of the axis, with a Singapore and Southeast Asia filter.

## Retrieval, prediction and refutation outperform engagement for its own sake

### The high-utility techniques are well established

Practice testing and distributed practice are the only two techniques Dunlosky and colleagues rated high-utility ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Testing-effect meta-analyses give **g = 0.50** ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)), **g = 0.61** ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)) and about 0.50 for classroom quizzing ([Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)). Retrieval transfers to new formats and contexts (d ≈ 0.40), and transfers better when questions are elaborate and include feedback ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)). In medical education, retrieval beat restudy at six months in residents ([Larsen, Butler & Roediger 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03518.x)). Spaced e-mailed cases improved medical students' retention with **d = 1.01 and 0.73** at 6–11 months ([Kerfoot et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17209889/)), though a multi-site follow-up helped one topic pair but not another ([Kerfoot & Brotschi 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614145/)). Optimal spacing gaps run at roughly 10–20% of the retention interval ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)). For a block of about 12 weeks, that implies review gaps of one to two weeks for core concepts. Anki deserves caution. The best-known study links roughly **1,700 extra unique cards to about one extra Step 1 point**, and it is correlational ([Deng et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673073)). A later retrospective cohort found no significant association at all ([IJMS 2023](https://ijms.pitt.edu/IJMS/article/view/1549)). No study has compared conceptual with factual flashcards in medical students. The worry that rote fact cards crowd out schema building is an inference from transfer research.

### Effortful generation helps when novices can succeed at it

Generating answers produces about **d = 0.40**, with larger effects at longer delays ([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441)). Failed attempts followed by feedback still improve learning ([Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729); [Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496)). Prediction works through curiosity and prediction error, so it needs feedback ([Brod 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642250/)). Conditions that slow performance during learning often improve retention, and learners mistake fluency for learning ([Soderstrom & Bjork 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)). Productive failure, meaning problem-solving before instruction, beat instruction-first with **g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51]** across 53 studies. The benefit concentrated on conceptual knowledge and transfer and depended on design fidelity ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf)). In 40 first-year pharmacy students, productive failure matched direct instruction on knowledge and beat it on preparation for future learning ([Steenhof et al. 2019](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/productive-failure-as-an-instructional-approach-to-promote-future/)). The limit is set by novice cognition. Minimally guided discovery is less effective for novices ([Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1)), and PBL showed a small negative trend on knowledge ([Dochy et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0959-4752(02)00025-7)). The workable synthesis is short "try first" problems that students can answer by reasoning from cell biology, followed at once by explicit explanation. A good example asks where an organism with oxygen-sensitive enzymes will grow in an oxygen gradient. A bad one asks students to predict a toxin's name.

### Refutation is the best-evidenced tool against the resistance misconception

A pre-registered meta-analysis of 76 studies and 294 effect sizes found a consistent advantage for refutation texts over standard text that no moderator overturned ([Danielson et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2024.2365628)). An earlier synthesis reports **g = 0.41**, an attribution that needs verifying ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z)). In the one microbiology-specific trial, refutation readings cut agreement with the teleological resistance misconception **from 59% to 21%**. A variant that explained *why the intuition is tempting* cut it **from 60% to 7%**. Yet a single reading did not reliably change how students explained resistance in their own words, and the authors recommend pairing refutation with active learning ([Pickett et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1128/jmbe.00220-21)). Constructed-response questions expose "mixed" models that multiple choice hides ([Uhl et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.20-06-0122)). There is also a warning for authors: instructors used anthropic or teleological language in every one of 90 sampled biology classes ([Betz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.19-04-0076)). The book's own prose must avoid "bacteria learn to resist".

Analogies cut both ways. Glynn's Teaching-With-Analogies model requires stating where an analogy breaks down ([Glynn 2008](http://osu-wams-blogs-uploads.s3.amazonaws.com/blogs.dir/548/files/2010/10/Glynn2008MakingScienceConceptsMeaningful.pdf)). Without that step, students over-map, for example assuming bacteria have sexes because conjugation was taught as "mating" ([CBE—LSE](https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.22-07-0142)). Critiques of war metaphors for host–microbe relations are largely argument, not experiment ([NASEM workshop](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK57060/)), and analysis of COVID-19 communication disputes the claim that such metaphors are inherently harmful ([PMC8022596](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8022596/)).

### Engagement formats mostly buy motivation, not measured learning

Interactive doing is associated with learning far more strongly than reading. In CMU Open Learning Initiative courseware, completed activities predicted outcomes about **six times more strongly than pages read** and three times more than videos watched ([Koedinger et al. 2015](https://cdl.ucf.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Doer-Effect.pdf)). The causal claims come from the developers and their commercial partner ([VitalSource](https://research.vitalsource.com/publications/the-doer-effect-at-scale-investigating-correlation-and-causation-across-seven-courses)). Active learning in STEM raised exam scores by about 0.47 SD, and traditional lecturing made failure 1.5 times more likely ([Freeman et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111)). Building concept maps (**g = 0.72**) beat studying expert maps (g = 0.43) ([Schroeder et al. 2018](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)).

The format-specific microbiology evidence is much thinner. Case-based learning reliably raises engagement, but its knowledge effects are mixed. One prospective study found **higher attendance (P = .008) but no test difference (P = .98)** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/379149007_Impact_of_case-based_learning_approach_in_clinical_microbiology_on_second-year_students_A_prospective_observational_study)). In a Brazilian microbiology PBL, students complained about **missing guiding questions and the lack of a clear final resolution** ([Vieira 2025](https://academic.oup.com/femsle/article/doi/10.1093/femsle/fnaf075/8217237)), two faults a branching e-book case can design out. Microbiology games are almost uniformly enjoyed. An AMR escape room scored 90.5% before class, 82.1% after the case and 90.5% after the escape room: no net gain ([OFID 2018](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/ofid/ofy210.1144)). The best independent virtual-lab study found Labster pre-lab simulation *equivalent* to face-to-face tutorials ([Makransky et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0155895)). Sketchy and Picmonic retention claims (Picmonic advertises retention gains of up to 331%) are vendor-generated, with no source study shown ([Picmonic](https://www.picmonic.com/microbiology)). These products also encode the organism → disease → drug catalogue this block avoids, and they raise equity concerns ([PMC6466489](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6466489/)). Interest matters too. Students exposed to case-based or research learning report more interest in microbiology, and almost three-quarters of ID entrants became interested before or during medical school. That evidence is self-report from 14 students at one US school ([Cervantes & Hong 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8439612/)). **The notes contain no evidence on LLM simulated-patient chats in any form**, so any use of them here is design inference.

## Design principles for the Quarto–Svelte e-book

### Organising framework: three layers that answer the same six questions

The evidence supports a three-layer frame (the integration is inference). Use **AMSMIC's foundational headings as the chapter skeleton**: bacteriology, virology, mycology and parasitology, host–pathogen interaction, and general principles of detection and control. Use **the ASM 2024 fundamental statements as the "big ideas" spine**, which keeps cell biology, evolution and ecology visible. Use **the damage-response framework as the cross-cutting lens** linking back to general pathology and across to immunology. Within that frame, every organism group is interrogated with the same fixed question set. This makes diversity read as "same questions, different answers" rather than a catalogue. The six questions are: *What is it built from? How does it make energy, and what does it need to grow? How does it copy itself and change? Where does it live, and how does it travel? How does damage arise, and how much is microbe versus host? What does it have that my cells lack?* The last question previews selective toxicity without naming drugs, and it turns "antibiotics don't kill viruses" from a rule into a consequence of cell biology students already know. The question set is an inference. Its parts rest on the Woods mechanism evidence, the ASM statements (FS6, FS11, FS29) and the misconception data.

### Sequencing: attribute toolkit first, contrast sets second, integration last

The proposed order follows CLT's pre-training and segmenting guidance, the expertise-reversal case for fading, and the NUS position after general pathology. The specific order is inference and has not been tested.

| Unit | Content (pathogen-light) | Signature interactive | Explicit bridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Scale and the microbial world | Size zoom from red cell to bacterium to virus; tree of life; most microbes harmless (FS22/28); Wolbachia as a "useful" bacterium | Log-scale zoom; sort organisms on a tree | Cell biology (organelles, ribosomes) |
| 2. Bacterial build and energy | Envelope, Gram stain as a window on the wall, spores, capsule; respiration vs fermentation; the oxygen spectrum | Gram-stain step simulator; thioglycollate predict-reveal; growth simulator (O₂, temperature) | Electron transport chain and ROS (cell biology) |
| 3. Bacterial genetics and change | Mutation, horizontal gene transfer, selection on pre-existing variation | Selection simulation on antibiotic plates; refutation unit on "the body becomes resistant" | Seeds pharmacology (resistance) |
| 4. Viruses | Structure, genome types, the replication cycle, envelope, obligate intracellular dependence | Segmented replication-cycle stepper; (+)/(−)RNA predict-reveal; sanitiser prediction | Host-cell machinery (cell biology); antiviral logic |
| 5. Fungi and parasites | Eukaryotic microbes; yeast vs mould; protozoa vs helminths; why few unique targets | Side-by-side comparison widget; image sorter | Selective toxicity as a recurring question |
| 6. Host–microbe interaction | Colonisation, microbiota, DRF outcomes, MISTEACHING, signature virulence strategies (toxin vs invasion vs intracellular survival) | DRF slider (microbe vs host contribution to damage); skeleton concept map LPS → endotoxin → inflammation | General pathology (necrosis, inflammation, granuloma); immunology |
| 7. Transmission, outbreaks and One Health | Reservoirs, routes, carriage, incubation, zoonosis; Snow, Semmelweis, Nipah, SARS 2003, HFMD, melioidosis | SIR/agent-based model; outbreak investigation branching case | Public health; organ-block presentations |
| 8. Principles of detection and control | How microbes are seen, grown and detected *in principle*; sterilisation vs disinfection; vaccination concept | Erroneous-example profile hunt; cumulative interleaved review | Organ-block diagnosis; pharmacology |

Units 2–5 should block their expository explanations, then end in interleaved practice that mixes in earlier units. From Unit 4 onward, cumulative quizzes should resurface bacteria-versus-virus discriminations. History works as a chapter-opening problem narrative (Snow for water-borne transmission, Semmelweis for contact transmission, Pasteur and Lister for sterilisation and antisepsis), not as decoration. Story-based approaches reliably raise interest, but their retention data are weak ([storytelling review](https://www.cedtech.net/download/the-utility-of-storytelling-strategies-in-the-biology-classroom-6210.pdf)).

### Presenting organisms: profiles and contrasts, never pages

The proto-script "microbe profile" is an inference from illness-script and encapsulation theory, not a tested intervention. Every exemplar gets the same fixed template with five slots: **identity and structure** (group, envelope, genome, morphology); **lifestyle and niche** (oxygen relationship, reservoir, general transmission, intracellular or extracellular); **mechanism of harm** (one or two signature strategies, linked to general pathology); **general consequence** (for example, toxin-mediated without invasion, versus pyogenic and invasive); and a **contrast partner** (the organism it is most easily confused with, and the attribute that separates them). The template's slots map onto the enabling-conditions, fault and consequences structure that organ blocks will later fill with sites, diagnosis and treatment. Profiles should appear in pairs or triads, not one per page. Students should complete profiles before they see expert versions (generation; construct beats study), and later chapters should fade them from worked to partial to blank. Colour coding should stay constant across the book (one colour always means envelope, another oxygen) so that diagrams, profiles and comparison grids share a visual vocabulary. This follows Mayer's signalling principle, which is lab-based.

### Matching interactive formats to goals

| Format | Best-fit goal | Evidence status | Design rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interactive diagrams and simulations | Mechanism (envelope, replication cycle, growth, transmission) | Segmenting/pre-training lab evidence; virtual labs ≈ tutorials; doer effect correlational | Predict → run → explain; segment to one idea per step; label in place, no decorative art |
| Predict-before-reveal prompts | Confronting misconceptions (oxygen, envelope, spores, RNA genome sense) | Generation d ≈ 0.40; prediction needs feedback; no microbiology study | Answerable by reasoning from cell biology; log predictions for analytics |

| Refutation boxes | The resistance cluster, "vaccines must cause disease", viruses as cells | Refutation meta-analyses; one microbiology RCT | Name the misconception, explain why it is tempting, then require a free-text explanation |
| Skeleton concept maps | Causal chains (LPS → inflammation; genome → replication strategy) | Constructing g = 0.72; microbiology studies quasi-experimental | One map per dimension, not per organism; auto-check node-link-node triples |
| Branching MCQ cases | Consolidation and integration | Worked examples help novices; CBL engagement strong, knowledge mixed | Place after the toolkit; worked → completion → full; investigation and mechanism questions, not diagnosis; explicit resolution |
| Spaced-repetition flashcards | Long-term retention of core concepts | Spacing strong; Anki evidence correlational | Why/how, discrimination and prediction cards; cloze only for pre-training vocabulary; small deck; Anki export |
| LLM chats | Transmission reasoning and history of science | **No evidence in the notes** | Re-scope from "patient" to exposure-history interview (where, who, when, what contact) or historical figure (Snow, Semmelweis); fixed fact sheet; refuse diagnosis and treatment; log transcripts |

The simulated-patient format deserves a specific caution. A patient encounter naturally pulls toward diagnosis and treatment, which this block defers. Its foundation-level value lies in eliciting **enabling conditions**: exposure, contacts, incubation, reservoir and carrier status. Typhoid-Mary-style carriage or an outbreak-contact interview teaches those without clinical reasoning. Because no evidence base exists, this format should be treated as an evaluated pilot, not a core component.

### What to avoid

Several pitfalls follow directly from the evidence. **Long scrolling organism tables** are studying, not comparing, and they add extraneous load. **Organism → facts flashcards** waste the testing effect, because retrieval transfers best when questions are elaborate. **Interleaving the first explanation of a concept** buys nothing, since expository text shows no reliable interleaving benefit. **First-contact cases before the attribute toolkit** ignore novices' need for guidance. **Vendor mnemonic models** encode the deferred catalogue. **Teleological prose** such as "bacteria want to resist" matches the misconception students already hold. And **the SOD/catalase story stated as settled** teaches an explanation current work has superseded. Two further risks are inferences rather than findings: **overclaiming the microbiome**, which no outcome evidence supports and which invites over-correction, and **games without a retrieval core**, where the evidence shows high satisfaction but no measured gain.

## Conclusion

The question is usually framed as concepts versus taxonomy, but the evidence points elsewhere. AMSMIC itself keeps organism groups as headings, and the most-cited innovative courses keep the Gram-positive/Gram-negative/viruses/parasites skeleton. What changes is the **unit students are asked to learn and retrieve**. In a bug parade that unit is the organism. In the design supported here it is the attribute dimension and the near-neighbour contrast. Organisms become worked examples of combinations students already understand, and the organ blocks can add pathogens to an existing schema rather than to an empty list. That reframing also gives the e-book an advantage no lecture series has. Prediction logs, two-tier quiz justifications and pre/post inventory items can generate exactly the evidence the field lacks: the first data on Singapore medical students' microbiology misconceptions, and potentially the first comparison of concept-organised and catalogue-organised foundation teaching in medicine.

The main risk is not that students will learn too little microbiology. It is that engaging formats will quietly re-import what the block defers. Branching cases drift toward diagnosis, simulated patients toward treatment, mnemonic images toward organism → drug facts, and enthusiastic microbiome content toward claims the evidence does not support. Every interactive component should pass one test before it ships: does it make students retrieve, compare or predict a mechanism they can reason about from cell biology and general pathology? The components that pass are the ones the evidence actually supports.
